NAXOS ARCHAEOLOGY

Prehistoric & Paleolithic

Stelida — The oldest archaeological find on the island, and among the oldest in Greece, was located here: pyrite processing workshops from the Middle and Upper Paleolithic period, dating to roughly 80,000–10,000 BC.

Grotta (Prehistoric Settlement) — A larger and much more developed settlement, with meticulously built houses and numerous pottery items, was discovered at Grotta, where Chora stands today. Naxos emerges from archaeological research as one of the most important centers of the Proto-Cycladic civilization.

 

Early Cycladic (3rd Millennium BC)

Aplomata Cemetery — This site has yielded Early Cycladic pyxides of marble and schist, as well as Mycenaean grave goods including a jar decorated with an octopus design. A Roman-era mosaic floor depicting a Nereid on a bull was also found here.

Kamini Cemetery — A Mycenaean jug decorated with a circular dance scene, and four golden plaques from a child burial, were retrieved from this cemetery.

Korphi t'Aroniou — An excavated site in eastern Naxos that has contributed finds now housed in the Archaeological Museum.

Tsikalario — Golden jewelry from tombs here has been dated to the Geometric period.

 

Mycenaean Period (c. 1300 BC)

Grotta (Mycenaean City) — Excavation work has led to the discovery of part of Naxos's Mycenaean-era capital, dated to 1300 BC, on the northern side of the harbor, below Mitropoleos Square. The section discovered constitutes part of the ancient agora and settlement. Some ancient constructions are seen submerged in the sea today.

Mycenaean Tomb at Chosti — The Mycenaean tomb at Chosti is an important and rare monument in the Cyclades, being one of only three vaulted tombs discovered in the region.

Mycenaean Tombs at Aplomata / Eggares — In the Eggares region, near the area of Aplomata, visitors can see the remains of Mycenaean tombs.

 

Archaic Period (7th–6th Century BC)

The Portara (Temple of Apollo) — Construction began around 530 BC under the tyrant Lygdamis, intended to be grander than any other temple in Greece. The massive marble doorway is all that remains of this unfinished Temple of Apollo.

Temple of Demeter, Sangri — A Late Archaic Greek temple built around 530 BC, it represents one of the earliest buildings in the Ionic order and was constructed entirely of local marble.

Sanctuary of Dionysus at Yria — One of the most important sanctuaries of ancient Naxos, dedicated to Dionysus and possibly a female deity of nature, operated at Yria, south of Naxos Town, in the middle of the fertile plain.

Kouros of Apollonas — Located in the Kastraki region near the village of Apollonas, this unfinished statue is 10.45 metres long and dates to the 6th century BC.

Kouros of Flerio (Melanes) — Another unfinished kouros situated near the village of Melanes, dating to the 7th century BC and measuring 6.40 metres long.

Marble Quarries of Flerio — The area around Flerio, rich in thick surface layers of good quality marble, was the cradle of the development of Greek marble architecture and sculpture.

Marble Quarries of Apollonas — One of Naxos's two main marble quarrying areas in antiquity, the locale is replete with remnants of quarrying activity.

Gyroulas — An ancient sanctuary from which an offering vessel with inscription dated to the late 7th century BC has been recovered, now displayed in the Archaeological Museum.

Panormos — An excavated ancient site whose finds are now housed in the Archaeological Museum of Naxos.

 

Classical, Hellenistic & Roman Periods

Ancient Aqueduct (Barou–Kaminia) — The entry and exit points of the underground tunnel of the ancient Naxos aqueduct were discovered at the positions of Barou and Kaminia respectively after copious excavations — an important feat of ancient engineering.

Aplomata Roman Villa — The site also yielded a Roman-era mosaic floor, fragments of which are displayed in the Archaeological Museum.

NAXOS BEACHES

West & Southwest Coast — The Main Beach Corridor

Agios Georgios — Credited with a Blue Flag beach-quality designation and located just a short distance from Chora, Agios Georgios rates as the best choice for those who do not wish to distance themselves from the capital. The northern side is particularly family-friendly, while the other end caters to nightlife enthusiasts.

Agios Prokopios — Considered one of the most beautiful beaches in Greece, located just 5 kilometres from Chora. This fine sandy beach stretches for about 1.5 kilometres and is naturally protected by the promontory of Stelida, which shelters it from the north winds.

Agia Anna — One of the most beautiful beaches on Naxos. The northern section with its golden sand is very touristy, with plenty of places to eat and drink. It is much quieter beyond the pier, where the rounded bay is unspoilt, with turquoise blue water meeting a powdery white sandy beach.

Maragas — A nudist-friendly beach located on the small peninsula separating Agia Anna and the main Plaka strip, about 7 km south of Chora.

Plaka — The famous Plaka is a long, sprawling strip of white sand with sand dunes behind it, one of the most beautiful beaches on Naxos. Here you can enjoy the lovely water and a view of Paros. Nearly 4 km long, even in midsummer large stretches remain deserted. The southern part has tamarisk trees providing natural shade.

Kedros — Part of the Plaka continuum, a quieter, less-organized stretch between Maragas and the main Plaka area, popular for its seclusion.

Orkos — Between Plaka and Mikri Vigla, Orkos is a lesser-known and often quieter beach with coarser golden sand than its neighbours, small rocky promontories creating sheltered coves, and a succession of intimate inlets.

Mikri Vigla — A heavenly beach of the Cyclades, with shallow entry into the crystal-clear sea. The hilly cape shields it from the north winds, making this grey sandy beach perfect for families. The scenery with its large granite rocks is simply stunning. It is also the most famous windsurfing spot on Naxos.

Sahara (Limanakia) — Located in the Mikri Vigla area, it is an amazing place and well protected from the summer wind, making it an excellent beach for families with children.

Kastraki — Kastraki Beach connects seamlessly to Mikri Vigla and stretches past the Paradise Kastraki Tavern all the way to Glyfada Beach. Along nearly 4 km, Naxos shows its unspoiled side — sand dunes reach far into the hinterland, and only around the settlement of Kastraki are there holiday resorts.

Glyfada — The southernmost reach of the Kastraki dune system, popular with advanced kitesurfers.

 

Southwest Peninsula — Alyko & Surrounds

Alyko — A quiet, undeveloped peninsula on the island's southwest coast, home to multiple beaches and the largest cedar forest in Greece. The main beach has golden sand and clear water, with rocky outcrops offshore where you can spot fish or even the occasional Loggerhead sea turtle while snorkeling.

Hawaii Beach — Probably the most famous section of Alyko, known for its dramatic red cliffs and narrow sandy cove. Between Alyko and Hawaii is an abandoned hotel that local graffiti artists have turned into an open-air gallery.

Aliko Agios Georgios — A second, more sheltered beach within the Alyko peninsula, screened by cedar forest and largely unorganized.

 

Southern Coast

Pyrgaki — An enormous sandy beach with a strong sense of wide open space, located on the southwestern coast, with views of the nearby island of Iraklia and Ios further beyond.

Agiassos — The southernmost beach reachable by paved road. Characterized by fine golden sand and a completely unspoilt environment, it offers plenty of space even during the busiest periods. The turquoise and crystal-clear waters slope gently, with small coves on the sides allowing corners of absolute privacy.

Spedo — Located on the island's southern coast, an unspoiled beach with immaculately clean waters in an area graced by rare natural beauty.

 

Eastern (Aegean-Facing) Coast

Psili Ammos — A spellbinding beach on the island's southeastern coast, spread before a juniper tree forest and offering a view of neighbouring Koufonissi. Its name means "fine sand," which aptly describes its character.

Klido — Discovered south of Psili Ammos, several bays with grey sandy beaches invite you to cool off in the deep blue Aegean. Completely unorganized — a spot for those seeking undisturbed solitude.

Panermos (Panormos) — Located about 1.5 hours from Naxos Town, with a true "end of the world" atmosphere. The Panormos Beach Bar and Mitatos Tavern offer refreshments under palm trees. Sheltered by the gulf at the island's southeastern coast, the harbor is almost always windless.

Amitis — A calm, clean beach about 20 minutes' drive from Chora, on the eastern side of the island, largely off the tourist radar.

 

Northern Coast

Agii Theodori — Pebble-covered and unspoilt, located on the island's northern coast. The Byzantine church of Agios Theodoros, from which it takes its name, is an added attraction.

Agios Fykas — A small but gorgeous beach located on the northeast coast of Naxos.

Apollonas Beach — A small sandy beach in the fishing village of Apollonas in the island's far north, bordered by tavernas and within walking distance of the Kouros of Apollonas.

 

NAXOS FOOD

Naxos Town (Chora)

To Elleniko — A family-owned, traditional Greek restaurant with cozy indoor seating or an open-air garden patio. The menu highlights the best local vegetables, cheeses, and meats prepared in classic Naxian and Greek styles, featuring plenty of fresh seafood, locally raised goat, and vegetarian options. Portions are enormous.

Doukato — A romantic restaurant set in the courtyard of an old monastery, with tables arranged on cobblestones under a large tree, surrounded by flowers and climbing vines. Widely regarded as the best dining experience in Chora for a special evening.

Boulamatsis — A Greek taverna located in the port of Naxos Town, serving a wide variety of local traditional dishes based on local family recipes, with home-cooked meals of both meat and seafood dishes, as well as vegetarian options. Known for its sunset views over the water.

Maro's Taverna (Stis Maros) — A local, no-frills restaurant in Naxos serving huge portions of traditional Greek food, located a short distance from Naxos Castle and Agios Georgios beach. Half portions are available on request, making it great value.

Scirocco Restaurant — A family-owned Greek taverna that has been a staple in Naxos for almost 20 years, located in the lively Protodikiou Square. Known for its friendly service and wonderful appetizers. Also serves Italian dishes like carbonara.

Barabbas — A traditional taverna near the port, popular with locals. Open for dinner only, specializing in fish and slow-cooked meats — any pork dish on the menu comes highly recommended.

Ta Filarakia — A small, no-frills taverna in Naxos Town where many locals hang out for a meal or a drink. The food is good and the authentic local atmosphere makes up for the absence of a view.

Metaxi Mas — Located in the charming alleys of Palia Agora in Naxos Town, a beloved spot known for its mezedes and Naxian specialties in a traditional setting.

To Souvlaki tou Maki — The best souvlaki and gyros in Naxos, this tiny grill house offers locally raised meats, hand-cut fries made with famous Naxos potatoes, homemade wine, and Greek beer. Open noon to midnight year-round.

La Vigne — A wine bar offering a wide variety of wines from Greece and France, serving fusion cuisine combining Mediterranean, French, and Asian flavors. Located in a narrow alley in central Naxos Town, it provides a cozy and welcoming environment.

Typografio Restaurant — A well-regarded spot in Chora frequently cited among the town's top tables.

Barozzi Naxos Restaurant — Another consistently recommended Chora restaurant, known for its refined take on Greek cuisine.

Irini's Restaurant — Located along the waterfront of Naxos Town, in close distance to the port.

Capriccio Restaurant — Located on Port Street, open daily 13:30–23:00, with reservations suggested in high season.

Taverna Naxos — On the Chora waterfront, featuring a mix of Greek and Naxian fare alongside some Italian dishes, and an interesting Gin & Tonic menu.

Nostimon Hellas — Serves high-quality traditional foods made in creative ways on a lovely terrace, carefully prepared from the finest raw materials.

Hurmas — A newer addition opening in 2024, open all day for breakfast, lunch, dinner, and drinks, and also open year-round. Excellent food and wine selection, with a beautiful interior.

Naxos Apothecary — Highly rated by the Greeka team for its creative use of local Naxian produce in a stylish setting.

 

Agios Prokopios & Agios Georgios

Giannoulis — Highly popular at Agios Prokopios, known to draw queues in peak season. Fresh seafood and grills dominate the menu.

Nissaki Restaurant (at Nissaki Beach Hotel) — Best hotel restaurant in Naxos, located on Agios Georgios beach.

Thalasea — A beach bar-restaurant at Orkos bay with a laid-back surf attitude and great food.

 

Agia Anna

Taverna Paradiso — The most photographed taverna in Naxos — and perhaps all of Greece. The setting with chairs under trees right by the beach is iconic. Notable dishes include grilled meats, briam (mixed vegetables in tomato sauce), and gigantes (giant beans).

Palatia — Situated by the beach in a renovated space with Cycladic decoration, ideal for breakfast, lunch, a romantic dinner, or a family meal, with a relaxing sea view.

Faros tou Alykou — Offers organic dishes with a stunning beach view, including tender wild goat, grilled shrimp, Mediterranean salad, and an extensive wine selection. Guests can dine in the courtyard overlooking the ocean.

 

Plaka Beach

Nicolas & Maria's — About 10 tables three feet from the sea on Plaka beach. Dinner and sunset on the beach is described as magical. A reservation is strongly recommended.

Petrino Restaurant — Entirely built of natural stones, surrounded by greenery, with the Aegean Sea only steps away. Seafood dishes are a highlight.

Picasso on the Beach — Serving Mexican food since 2004, located on the seaside, offering every customer the chance to have a great meal overlooking the Aegean Sea.

Cedar Café — A gorgeous lounge café-bar and restaurant on Plaka beach offering an amazing sunset view, cocktails, and relaxed music.

Patrino Taverna — Near Nicolas & Maria's on Plaka, specializing in traditional Naxian dishes, offering a distinctly different experience from a standard taverna.

Yazoo Summer Bliss — The best beach club on Plaka beach.

O Paradisos — Literally by the sea between Agia Anna and Plaka. One showcase displays ready-made dishes, another local meats and fresh fish. The menu is rich and portions generous, with relatively original dishes such as tomato pie, mushroom pie, and pumpkin pie.

 

Kastraki & Southwest Beaches

Axiotissa (Kastraki) — Highly recommended for its fantastic setting, top-notch service, and a menu of original creations made with fresh organic products. Known for the best swordfish on the island, and a braised wild rabbit dish. Reservations are a must.

 

Melanes Village

Vassilis Tavern — In the most picturesque alley of Melanes, a place with fantastic food made from their own produce. If you want to try real local Naxos recipes, Vassilis is the best place for good food and hospitality.

 

Mountain Villages (Filoti, Halki, Apeiranthos, Koronos)

O Vasilis (Filoti area) — A favorite among locals, offering the most authentic Naxian food experience on the island. The family breeds and farms their own roosters, rabbits, lamb, and pigs. Perched on a hillside garden terrace, the restaurant offers views over the village, blue-domed church, and mountains. Open daily year-round.

Potērou (Damarionas, near Halki & Filoti) — Offers tasty dishes made from local raw materials in a unique, picturesque environment in the heart of Naxos.

Amorginos (Apeiranthos) — A local spot in the distinctive marble village of Apeiranthos, offering authentic mountain food alongside the charm of a village frequented by traditional musicians.

Apeiranthos Mountain Lamb Taverna — In the stunning marble village of Apeiranthos, this taverna has perfected slow-roasted mountain lamb. The potatoes, cooked in the lamb's juices, are transcendent, and the setting overlooking terraced hillsides completes the experience.

Good quality food is also found in the most unlikely inland places, far from the island's alluring shores — Tripodes, Halki, Filoti, Apeiranthos, Koronos, Potamia, and Eggares are all such examples.

 

Apollonas (North)

Matina & Stavros Taverna — A waterfront family taverna in the fishing village of Apollonas serving some of the island's best seafood in a setting that feels authentically Greek.

The locals converted this seaside village into a quaint resort, with traditional Greek tavernas laid along its small port.

 

NAXOS FESTIVITIES

 

Naxos's festival calendar is remarkably dense, reflecting the island's deep rootedness in both Orthodox Christianity and a much older, Dionysian spirit of communal celebration. For Fab Naxos Villas content, the Bazeos Festival, Dionysia, Demetria, and the major August panigyria offer strong editorial and SEO angles — particularly for guests planning visits around cultural experiences rather than purely beach holidays.

 

Winter & Pre-Lenten Season

Christmas & Epiphany (December–January): Christmas on Naxos follows the broader Greek tradition, with midnight liturgies and the custom of children singing carols (kalanda) in exchange for small gifts. The feast of Agios Nikolaos, patron saint of sailors, is observed on December 6th in Kynidaros village. On Epiphany (January 6th), the Blessing of the Waters ceremony takes place in Naxos harbor, where the local bishop casts a cross into the sea.

Naxos Carnival (Apokries — February/March): Carnival on Naxos traces its roots to the ancient cult of Dionysus, the god of wine and theater, with Christianity silently incorporating these dominant celebrations into the religious calendar as a final wild enactment before the 40-day Lenten fast. The island's carnival is one of the liveliest in the Cyclades, with masquerade processions, satirical floats, traditional music, and dancing in the streets of Chora and the villages. It culminates on Clean Monday (Kathara Deftera) with outdoor feasts.

 

Spring — Holy Week & Easter

Orthodox Easter (March/April — moveable feast): Easter is the most significant religious and cultural celebration in Greece. On Naxos, the celebrations are deeply felt, particularly in the mountain villages. Holy Week includes nightly church services, the solemn Epitaphios procession on Good Friday — where the bier of Christ is carried through village streets accompanied by chanting and candlelight — and the midnight Resurrection service on Holy Saturday with its dramatic lighting of candles from the Holy Flame. Easter Sunday is marked by communal lamb roasts, red-egg cracking, and all-day feasting.

Panagia Argokiliotissa — Good Friday, Koronos: The feast of Panagia Argokiliotissa takes place on Holy Friday, before Easter, at Koronos village. It is known as the most significant feast of Naxos, where pilgrims worship the miraculous icon of the Holy Virgin. Locals celebrate with wine, delicacies, and dances.

Agia Irini — May 5th, Kynidaros: The feast day of Agia Irini, celebrated in Kynidaros village, marks the beginning of the summer panigyri calendar.

Agios Thalalaeos — May 20th, Agios Thalalaeos village: On the 20th of May, the feast day of Agios Thalalaeos is celebrated in Agios Thalalaeos village.

 

Summer Religious Festivals (Panigyria)

Naxos is the largest island of the Cyclades and has plenty of religious festivals known since antiquity. Once a year, the villages and settlements of Naxos celebrate their patron saints with traditional dances and music, local delights, and wine served to the people. Bagpipe instruments (tsabouna), violins, and drums (doubakia) are an unfailing occurrence in the squares and courtyards of churches during celebrations and festivities.

Agii Apostoli — June 29th–July 1st, Melanes: The feast day of Agioi Apostoloi is celebrated with a three-day festival in Melanes village. One of the more extended panigyria on the island.

Agia Kyriaki — July 7th, Potamia: The feast day of Agia Kyriaki is celebrated in Potamia village.

Agios Prokopios — July 8th, Agios Prokopios: The feast day of Agios Prokopios is celebrated in the namesake village. 

Agios Nikodemos — July 14th, Chora & Glinado: Locals celebrate the patron saint of Naxos, Agios Nikodemos Agioritis. Celebrations take place mainly in Chora and, after the church ceremonies, a procession of the icon starts throughout the streets. This is among the most prominent celebrations in Naxos Town itself. 

Agia Marina — July 17th, Aggidia & Koronos: The feast of Agia Marina is celebrated in two villages simultaneously.

Agia Anna — July 25th, Agia Anna: The feast of Agia Anna at the beach village of the same name — a celebration that blends religious observance with the festive atmosphere of one of Naxos's most visited coastal settlements.

Agia Paraskevi — July 26th, Kynidaros:The feast of Agia Paraskevi in Kynidaros.

Agios Panteleimon — July 27th, Agersani: The feast of Agios Panteleimon in the village of Agersani.

Sotiros (Transfiguration of the Savior) — August 6th, Glinado, Damarionas & Kourounochori: The feast of the Transfiguration of Christ (Metamorphosi tou Sotiros) is observed simultaneously in multiple villages, each holding its own panigyri.

Assumption of the Virgin Mary (Dekapentavgoustos) — August 15th: A major public holiday and religious celebration across Greece, with religious services and large village panigyria featuring live traditional music, dancing, and communal meals — particularly vibrant in mountainous villages. On Naxos, the main celebrations are centred on Filoti, the island's largest inland village. Ferries fill up rapidly around this date. 

Enniamera Panagia — August 23rd, Tripodes: The "Nine Days After the Assumption" feast at Tripodes — a distinctly Naxian tradition that extends the Assumption festivities by nine days.

Agios Ioannis — August 29th, Apeiranthos, Apollonas & Agersani: The feast of the Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, celebrated simultaneously in three villages on different ends of the island.

 

Autumn Religious Observances

Theoskepasti — September 8th, Komiaki & Potamia: A great religious celebration takes place at the feast of Theoskepasti in the church of Paros Theoskepasti in Komiaki and Potamia. September 8th also marks the Nativity of the Virgin Mary.

Panagia Drossiani — September 8th, Moni:One of the oldest and most revered churches in the Cyclades, the Byzantine church of Panagia Drossiani at Moni celebrates its feast day on the same date, drawing pilgrims from across the island.

Timios Stavros (Holy Cross) — September 14th, Tripodes & Moni: The feast of Timios Stavros is held at Tripodes and Moni simultaneously.

Agios Artemios — October 20th, Kynidaros: The feast of Agios Artemios at Kynidaros village.

Agios Nikolaos — December 6th, Kynidaros: The patron saint of sailors and travelers, observed in Kynidaros.

 

Cultural & Secular Festivals

Naxos Festival / Bazeos Tower Festival (June–September): The Naxos Festival has been organized every year since 2001, from June to September. It takes place at the Bazeos Tower, an exceptional 17th-century monument, about 2 km east of Sangri. The festival began as a daring and imaginative idea to transform the outstanding and historical monument into a temple of art, education, and culture. The main objective of the festival is to promote the local culture through cultural events, literature, exhibitions, theatrical and musical performances, and to encourage the intersection between national and international cultures. 

Axia Music Festival (Summer, Halki): The Axia Music Festival is a lovely event organized annually in Halki, highly appreciated by lovers of classical music and those interested in poetry. The festival hosts excellent musicians to make your nights in Naxos even more romantic.

Demetria Festival (First Week of August, Sangri): The Demetria event, a cultural institution on Naxos, is held every year during the first week of August at Sangri, a traditional village and the location of the archaeological site of the Temple of Demeter. It features mostly agrotourism stalls where visitors may try goods produced and grown on the blessed land of Naxos. Concerts and theatrical plays take place in the courtyard of Agios Eleftherios, a 17th-century monastery, while art exhibitions by established artists are held inside. Photography exhibitions take place along the village's delightful alleys.

Dionysia Festival (Early September): Dionysia is one of the best-known festivals on Naxos. It includes concerts, theatrical performances, art exhibitions, and a plethora of other events which are crowned with the wine feast in honor of the god Dionysus. The festival is held during the first weekend of September.

Raki Distillery Festival (September/October, Municipality of Drymalia): The Raki Distillery Festival is one of the most noteworthy events on the island, celebrated annually in September/October in the Municipality of Drymalia. Also known as the Rakitzo festival, it involves a great feast for the people — a celebration of the island's traditional distilled spirits, raki and kitron.

Naxathlon Triathlon (Annual): A sporting festival unique to Naxos, drawing participants from across Greece and abroad to compete across the island's varied terrain.

Harvest Festivals (September, various villages): In September, villages hold grape-picking celebrations with communal feasts and folk music. 

NAXOS NEAR-BY DESTINATIONS

 

Immediate Neighbors — Half-Day to Full-Day Reach

Paros — There are daily connections between Naxos and Paros, with more than one ferry per day during middle and high season. For the premium traveler, Naoussa is the draw: a charming and lively fishing village on the north of the island offering a blend of traditional and cosmopolitan atmosphere, with whitewashed houses, colorful boats, and chic bars and restaurants. Visitors can stroll the picturesque harbour, explore the Venetian castle, or visit the capital Parikia with its famous Ekatontapyliani church, or the traditional inland village of Lefkes. Crossing time is short — between 15 and 40 minutes depending on the ferry, though the 15-minute option lands at Piso Livadi in the south rather than the main towns.

Antiparos — Tiny Antiparos is accessed only via Paros, making it a natural pairing with a Paros day trip. The ferry from Paros to Antiparos is itself a popular short day trip, known for its laid-back, bohemian-chic reputation among the Greek elite and a celebrated sea cave.

Delos — The island where ancient Greeks believed Apollo was born is roughly a two-hour journey from Naxos via two separate public ferries, though tailored services can shorten this considerably, with about three hours to explore the UNESCO World Heritage archaeological site before continuing on. It was the birthplace of the Greek gods Apollo and Artemis. Almost always paired with Mykonos.

Mykonos — Delos and Mykonos are the most common choice when it comes to day trips from Naxos. A natural fit for travelers wanting a taste of Mykonos's glamour — Little Venice, the windmills, and beach-club culture — without committing to an overnight stay.

 

The Small Cyclades — For the Traveler Seeking Exclusivity

This cluster sits administratively under the same municipality as Naxos and represents some of the least-developed, most exclusive territory in the Aegean.

Koufonissi (Pano & Kato Koufonisi) — The most popular of the Small Cyclades, known for having some of the clearest, calmest, and bluest water in all the Greek islands. Pano Koufonisi's pristine beaches are considered among the best in the whole of the Cyclades. Once there, bikes can be hired, since cars are restricted and there is only one taxi on the island. A premium private sailing charter is the preferred mode of access for discerning travelers, avoiding the public ferry's crowds entirely.

Iraklia — A lesser-known small Cyclades island, time-stopped and largely undiscovered by mainstream tourism, ideal for travelers seeking genuine seclusion.

Schinoussa — Part of a full-day exploration of the Small Cyclades that typically combines swimming, snorkeling, sailing, and a traditional Greek lunch with wine. Quiet, minimally developed, with a handful of excellent small tavernas.

Donoussa — The most distant of the Small Cyclades group, a quiet and as-yet-untouched destination unknown to most tourists. The furthest reach for travelers who want to say they've genuinely gone off-grid.

 

Amorgos — The Dramatic Outlier

Just over an hour southeast of Naxos, Amorgos has a permanent population of around 2,000 residents, many farmers, fishermen, or in hospitality. It is only accessible by boat, which keeps tourist numbers low and is unlikely to change given its unusual terrain. It is home to one of the most incredible monasteries in Europe — the cliff-hung Monastery of Panagia Hozoviotissa — and a shipwreck featured in numerous films. Ferries run up to six times daily in summer, with the fastest services (Seajets, Hellenic Seaways) arriving via Katapola or Aegiali ports. For a premium traveler, this is the "discovery" excursion — dramatic, rugged, and largely untouched by mass tourism.

 

Santorini — The Marquee Detour

Naxos, often called the "Queen of the Cyclades," is one of the most favoured destinations in Greece, and a day trip between the two is a frequent ask. In summer, frequent daily ferries depart Santorini's Athinios Port from as early as 6:45am to around 3:30pm, with high-speed catamarans like SeaJets making the crossing in about an hour, though indirect routes stopping at islands like Amorgos can stretch to three hours. For travelers who want privacy and a tailored pace, renting a private cruise is an exceptional option for this route. Most travel advisors, however, recommend the reverse logic: pairing the dramatic, volcanic luxury of Santorini with the authentic, farm-to-table food and sandy beaches of Naxos as overnight stays rather than a rushed day trip.

 

Suggested Premium Itinerary Logic

A well-paced Cyclades itinerary often runs Mykonos → Naxos → Santorini or the reverse, with each island given two to four nights rather than treated as day trips. If a stay on Naxos is under five days, island hopping is generally discouraged, since the island itself is large enough to require several days to properly explore. One advantage Naxos holds for travelers building such an itinerary is logistics: the airport sits close to Naxos Town and the ferry port is walkable from the town centre, unlike Santorini or Mykonos where transfers between airport, port, and accommodation can take 20–30 minutes.

For a premium traveler, the highest-value framing is usually:

  • Half-day, high-glamour: Mykonos or Delos
  • Full-day, refined and quiet: Paros (Naoussa) + Antiparos
  • Full-day, exclusive/private charter: Koufonissi, via private sailing rather than public ferry
  • Multi-night extension, dramatic contrast: Amorgos or Santorini

 

This nicely complements the destination-guide pillar of the Fab Naxos Villas content plan — positioning Naxos not just as a destination in itself, but as the most logistically convenient and characterful base for exploring the wider Cyclades.

NAXOS' TRADITIONAL VILLAGES

 

Naxos Town & Immediate Surroundings

Chora (Naxos Town) — The main hub of the island and where the ferries dock at the port, home to the Venetian Castle, old town alleys, and the Portara nearby.

Galanado — A scenic, hidden traditional gem 5 km southeast of Chora, housing whitewashed buildings, a couple of hangout spots, and a plethora of fantastic churches. Built on a hill with impressive views of the Tragea valley and surrounding mountains, distinguished by its strategic location offering panoramic views all the way to Chora and the sea.

Glinado — A farming village established around 1650 AD, built on the slopes of a mountainside at an elevation of about 100m, offering a vista to the green fields below, 6km south of Naxos town with a population of about 500 inhabitants.

Agia Anna Village — A picturesque seaside gem on the southwestern coast, close to Chora with one of the best beaches on this side of the island, easily reached by bus from Naxos town.

 

The Tragea Valley — Heart of Traditional Naxos

Halki (Chalki) — A lovely, traditional village in the center of Naxos, 16 km southeast of Chora, that used to be the capital of the island and offers beautiful samples of architecture, galleries, and cafes. Formerly the administrative and commercial center of Naxos, located in the fertile region of Tragaia, known for its neoclassical buildings and Byzantine churches. It is home to the well-known Vallindras Distillery, where the traditional citrus liqueur has been produced since 1896. The church of Agios Georgios Diasoritis, an 11th-century Byzantine monument, is located nearby and is worth visiting for its unique frescoes.

Filoti — Nestled at the base of Mount Zas, Filoti is the largest main village after Naxos Town, known for its charming central square, cultural festivals, and beautiful hiking paths. It is actually made up of three settlements, with whitewashed cube houses built into the mountains in a semi-circle like an amphitheatre. It is a popular place for tourists with many cafes, tavernas, and shops in the square around the plane tree. Visitors can see the Tower of Barozzi and many churches in the area. It is also an ideal starting point for the climb to Mount Zas, the highest peak in the Cyclades.

Kaloxylos (Kaloksilos) — A beautiful, quiet little village located next to the famous village of Halki, 16 km southeast of Chora.

Damarionas — A traditional village considered mountainous although only at an altitude of 230 meters, beautifully built on the south side of the sunny Tragea valley, 16km from Naxos town.

Damalas — One of the smallest villages in Naxos, 15 km southeast of Chora, distinguished by its traditional, stone-built houses and culturally significant landmarks including the vintage oil press and pottery workshop.

Sangri — A mountain village 12 km southeast of Chora, famous for its Byzantine monasteries, Venetian towers, and the ancient Temple of Demeter. The name Sangri is a corruption of "Sainte Croix" (French for Holy Cross, or Timios Stavros in Greek), as the nearby monastery carries a French name.

Moni — Home to the Byzantine church of Panagia Drosiani, one of the oldest churches in the Cyclades, set on the upper slopes of the Tragea overlooking the valley below.

Tripodes — A quiet, central Tragea village, host to the Enniamera Panagia and Timios Stavros feasts.

Vourvouria / Akadimi — Small hamlets within the Tragea olive-grove belt, rarely visited by tourists, offering an unfiltered glimpse of agricultural village life.

 

Eastern Mountain Villages

Apeiranthos (Apiranthos) — A quaint mountain village in the eastern part of Naxos, 26 km east of Chora. Surrounded by lush greenery and keeping its elegant architectural style, Apeiranthos is still untouched by tourism. Also known as the "marble village," located in the mountains at an altitude of 650 meters on the slopes of Mount Fanari. The narrow, cobblestone streets and traditional stone houses create a truly unique atmosphere. It has been voted one of the prettiest villages in Europe. The culture and dialect here differ slightly from the rest of Naxos, as the original villagers settled here from Crete and the village retains a Cretan influence. Cars cannot enter the narrow streets, so a car park is used outside the village — and mules are sometimes parked there too.

Koronos — A mountainous village famous for its narrow, stepped streets, surrounding green valleys, and a history rooted in emery mining. One of the most picturesque mountain villages, nestled in a lush green valley with springs, characterized by narrow, uphill alleys, traditional houses, and small squares. The village has a long tradition of emery mining, and the Emery Museum offers interesting information about this activity.

Skado — One of the smallest villages of Naxos, located on the northeastern side of the island and surrounded by wild natural beauty, 29 km from Naxos Town. It is right after the village of Koronos on the eastern road axis of Stavros Keramoti to Apollonas.

Danakos — A mountain village on the eastern side of Naxos, 25 km southeast of Chora, and one of Naxos's oldest settlements. Small and quiet, on the slopes of Mount Zas, ideal for nature lovers and those seeking authenticity. It strongly preserves traditional Cycladic architecture. From here begins one of the most popular hiking trails to the summit of Za, offering panoramic views of the Aegean Sea. The village is known for its natural springs and the monastery of Fotodotis Christos.

Keramoti — Located right at the centre of Naxos Island at Stavros Keramotis, the largest intersection of rural roads on the island, roughly 25km from Chora.

Kinidaros (Kynidaros) — Located very close to the centre of Naxos island, about 15km east of Chora at an altitude of 400m. A village marked by several saints' feast days across the calendar year.

Potamia (comprising three settlements) — Comprising three smaller villages, Potamia is a lush, verdant area with abundant water sources, known for its fruit orchards, tranquil atmosphere, and picturesque scenery. It is a traditional village set on a hillside overlooking a wonderfully lush and cool valley, with a population of 300 inhabitants, dotted with monuments from Naxos's rich economic and cultural past, 9 km from Naxos town.

Kourounochori — A small village near Potamia and Glinado, traditionally agricultural and rarely visited outside locals attending its religious feast.

 

Northern & Coastal Villages

Eggares (Egares) — A small farming village in the midst of a fertile plain just a few kilometers north of Chora, with most of its 200 permanent residents being farmers. Known today for its historic olive press museum.

Galini — A traditional, quiet, and peaceful village in the verdant Galini–Eggares Valley.

Melanes — A green, spring-fed village in the valley below Halki, known for its kouros statues and as the site of ancient marble quarrying.

Kourounohori and Ano Potamia / Mesi Potamia / Kato Potamia — The three component settlements making up the broader Potamia valley complex.

Apollonas — A coastal village notable for its laid-back ambiance, featuring a small, delightful beach and home to an ancient Kouros statue, partially carved from local marble. A charming fishing village with a couple of great fish taverns and a sandy beach, 36 km northeast of Chora. A beautiful fishing village which has been inhabited since antiquity, probably on account of the marble quarry.

Agios Arsenios — One of the island's main villages in the northwestern interior, named for its patron saint.

Vivlos (Tripodes area) — One of the main villages of Naxos.

 

Southeastern Coastal & Fishing Villages

Moutsouna — A small fishing village on the eastern side of the island, secluded and providing spots for total privacy, with waterfront taverns serving the fresh catch of the day, 37 km from Chora. It is in fact the port of Apeiranthos, and in recent years has begun to develop as a tourist resort of note, partly due to its picturesque coves and the natural beauty of the whole southeastern coast.

 

Besides the largest town, Naxos Chora, there are more than 64 villages on the island, most of which are mountainous and known for their cooler climate and local cuisine — a foodie aspect tied to Naxos being the most fertile island of the Cyclades, with a good supply of water that makes it a major agricultural player, particularly for potatoes and dairy products, along with olive oil and citrus. According to Wikipedia, the main villages of the island are Filoti, Apiranthos, Vivlos, Agios Arsenios, Koronos, and Glinado. Today some of these villages are uninhabited or have very few inhabitants, mostly of the older generation, while others have populations of up to 2,000 people with considerable numbers of children — many having become major tourist attractions, especially during the high season when they host local festivals.

NAXOS, THROUGH THEIR EYES

 

Every traveler arrives at Naxos with a different set of questions. Here's what visitors from a few of our favorite corners of the world discovered once they got here — and where they stayed while they figured it out.

🇺🇸 AN AMERICAN IN NAXOS: WHY I SKIPPED SANTORINI AND DON'T REGRET IT

 

Every conversation about our trip to Greece started the same way. Someone would ask where we were going, and the moment I said "one of the smaller islands, not Santorini," I'd get the same slightly confused look. Santorini was the plan, for a while. It's the photo everyone already has in their head before they've booked a single flight.

Then a coworker, who'd been twice, said something that stuck with me: "Santorini is where you go to see Greece. Naxos is where you go to actually be there." I didn't fully understand what she meant until I was standing in the middle of Naxos Town at seven in the evening, watching the harbor go orange, with maybe thirty other people around instead of three hundred.

We'd done the research beforehand, the way you do when you're only getting one real vacation this year and don't want to waste it. Photos of Fira packed shoulder to shoulder at sunset. Reviews mentioning cruise ship days when the whole town changes character for a few hours. None of that is a knock on Santorini — it earned its reputation honestly. But it made us curious what a Greek island without that particular pressure would feel like.

The ferry in from Athens took a few hours, which gave us time to recalibrate expectations. What we found on the other end wasn't a lesser version of the famous islands. It was a working island that happened to be extraordinarily beautiful, rather than a beautiful island that had rearranged itself around tourism.

The Portara, the massive marble doorway standing alone at the harbor's edge, does the sunset thing everyone comes for — but there's room to actually stand there and look at it, rather than angling for a gap in a crowd. Chora's back alleys are a genuine maze, whitewashed and steep, and half the satisfaction was simply getting lost in them on purpose.

What surprised us most was the food. Not "surprisingly good for a small island" — just good, full stop, in the way that happens when a place still cooks primarily for the people who live there. Naxian potatoes turned out to be an actual point of local pride, not a marketing phrase. We ate better, and paid less, than we expected to almost every night.

The practical stuff, since Americans always want the practical stuff: renting a car is close to mandatory if you want to see beyond Chora, and it's worth it — the mountain villages are a different island entirely from the coast. Credit cards are widely accepted in town, less so in smaller villages, so keep some cash on hand. Tipping isn't the ritual it is at home; rounding up is appreciated, not expected.

Where we stayed mattered more than we'd planned for. We'd booked a villa almost as an afterthought, assuming it would function like a slightly larger hotel room. It didn't. It gave the trip a center of gravity — mornings on a terrace instead of a hotel breakfast room, a pool that meant the hottest part of the afternoon wasn't a problem to solve, evenings that felt like they belonged to us rather than to a schedule. It's a different kind of vacation when you have a door to come back to instead of a room number.

Santorini is still there, and it's still beautiful, and I don't think less of anyone who goes. But we got the version of Greece we didn't know we were looking for: quieter, less rehearsed, and somehow more memorable for exactly that reason. Santorini is where you take the photo. Naxos is where you remember the trip.

🇳🇱 WHAT A DUTCH FAMILY FOUND IN NAXOS THAT WE DIDN'T IN CRETE

 

This wasn't our first Greek island, or even our third. Crete, Rhodes, and a short stay on Kos are already behind us, so when we started planning this trip, the question wasn't "should we visit Greece" — it was "is there anywhere left that's actually worth the switch." Naxos kept coming up, usually described in the same slightly vague way: quieter, less developed, good for families. We wanted specifics before committing a week of our summer to it.

The flight and transfer logistics were straightforward, nothing more complicated than Crete had been. What was different showed up almost immediately: Naxos Town doesn't feel built for tourism the way parts of Crete's north coast now do. The harbor still functions as a harbor. The backstreets in Chora are narrow because that's how they were built centuries ago, not because someone designed them to look charming for photos.

We'd read that Naxos has real potential for cycling, given the terrain and quieter roads compared to somewhere like Malia, and that turned out to be true with a caveat: the coastal roads are manageable, but the interior climbs toward the mountain villages are genuinely steep. We rented e-bikes after the first day, which solved the problem entirely and let us reach villages like Filoti and Halki without the whole day being about the ride there.

Cost was the other thing we'd gone in wondering about, since we tend to budget carefully even on a trip we're happy to spend on. A week here worked out comparable to our last trip to Crete, sometimes less once we accounted for how much we cooked ourselves. The villa had a proper kitchen, not a kettle and a mini-fridge, and the morning market in town sold vegetables and cheese good enough that eating in didn't feel like a compromise.

To be fair about the trade-offs, since we'd rather be honest than glowing: the nightlife scene is much smaller than Crete's, if that's part of what you're after. English signage thins out fast once you're past the main tourist areas, and a few restaurants in the mountain villages only had menus in Greek, which was fine for us but worth knowing in advance. It is, in a real sense, a quieter island than the ones we'd visited before — that was the whole appeal, but it's worth going in with the right expectations.

For the kids, the shallow, sheltered stretch at Agia Anna solved the "is it safe to just let them swim" question on day one, and the villa's own pool covered the rest of the days when the beach felt like too much effort. Having that pool, plus the extra space a villa gives a family that a hotel room simply can't, made a bigger difference to how relaxed the week felt than we expected going in.

Would we recommend switching from Crete? Depends what you're chasing. If it's nightlife and a well-worn tourist infrastructure, stay with Crete. If it's the version of a Greek island that still runs on its own schedule rather than the tourist season's, Naxos earned its place on our list. We're already talking about which mountain village to base ourselves near next time.

🇸🇬 A FIRST-TIMER'S GUIDE TO NAXOS: WHAT SURPRISED ME MOST

 

Greece was always somewhere I'd get to eventually, on one of those trips that requires enough vacation days stacked together to justify the flight. When it finally happened, it was built around three weeks moving through Europe, with five days on Naxos in the middle — a name I'd only come across because a friend had visited the year before and wouldn't stop talking about it.

Coming from Singapore, where everything runs efficiently and on time almost by design, I'd braced myself for the opposite. What I found instead was a different kind of order — slower, less visible, but real. Ferries left when they said they would. Our host had answered every question before we'd even thought to ask it: how to get from the port, where the nearest supermarket was, which taverna did takeout on the one night we didn't feel like going out.

The anxiety I hadn't admitted to myself beforehand was about navigating a small island without much English signage. That fear mostly dissolved by the second day. Naxos Town has enough English in the places that matter — menus, ferry counters, pharmacies — and where it didn't, pointing and smiling covered the rest. The harder adjustment was simply the pace: shops closing for a few hours in the afternoon, dinner not really starting until nine, an unhurried rhythm that took active effort to stop fighting against.

Visually, it was more rewarding than I'd expected from photos alone. The Portara at golden hour is the obvious one, and it deserves the reputation. Less expected was the walk through Chora's old town, where the whitewashed alleys twist enough that you lose your sense of direction within two turns, and every corner has a different blue door or bougainvillea vine worth stopping for. The Temple of Demeter, out past the vineyards, had almost no one else there the afternoon we visited, which felt like a rare thing anywhere in Europe in summer.

Food ended up being the most consistent highlight, day after day. Not "Greek food" as a general idea, but specific things I hadn't heard of before arriving — the local potatoes, a particular sharp cheese, small fried fish eaten whole. Ordering became a small research project each night, and it never once disappointed.

Staying at a villa rather than a hotel made the whole trip feel more manageable than I'd expected for a first visit. There was a real sense of being looked after — someone to message if anything was unclear, a private space to retreat to after a long day of sightseeing, none of the impersonal feeling I'd worried a small Greek island might have for a first-time visitor from somewhere as different as Singapore.

Five days wasn't enough, in the end. It was enough to know I'd go back, and enough to understand why my friend wouldn't stop talking about it. If you're weighing whether a small Greek island is too much of an unknown for a first trip to Greece, I'd say it's exactly the opposite — it's the version of Greece that makes the rest of the unknown feel manageable too.

🇩🇪 WHAT I LOOK FOR IN A GREEK VILLA (AS SOMEONE WHO'S PICKY ABOUT QUALITY)

 

Before booking anything, I always check the same things, in roughly the same order: is the property actually as photographed, is the construction solid or just dressed up for the camera, and does the host respond like someone who takes the place seriously. Naxos passed on all three counts, which is a large part of why I'm writing about it at all.

I'd read, more than once, that Naxos was "less touristy" than the well-known islands, and I wanted to verify that rather than take it at face value. It held up. Naxos Town in the evening has a real crowd, but not the density of Santorini's caldera-side paths in high season. More telling was the interior of the island — villages like Apeiranthos and Filoti operate almost entirely on their own terms, with tourism as a visible but secondary layer over daily life, not the main economy dressed up as a village.

The hiking exceeded what I'd expected from a beach-holiday island. Trails around Mount Zeus range from a manageable afternoon walk to a genuinely demanding full-day route, with almost none of the crowding you'd find on comparable trails in the Alps during summer. I'd pack proper boots, not sandals with good intentions, and start early enough to avoid the midday heat on the exposed sections.

On the villa itself: construction quality was the first thing I checked on arrival, and it was reassuring. Thick stone walls that actually kept the interior cool without needing air conditioning running constantly, which matters both for comfort and for the electricity bill neither of us wanted to think about on holiday. The photos we'd been shown matched what we found, down to details like the kitchen counter material and the actual size of the pool — not a wide-angle lens trick.

Food provenance is something I pay attention to at home, and it carried over naturally here. The cheese at the weekly market came from a producer whose name the vendor could tell us on request. The potatoes, which Naxos is genuinely known for regionally rather than as a tourist-brochure claim, showed up on nearly every menu we tried, and by the third night we understood why locals bother mentioning them at all.

Precision on the practical details, since that's what I'd want if I were reading this beforehand: the best window to visit is late May to mid-June or September, avoiding both the peak heat and the more crowded weeks of July and August. A week, realistically, costs less than equivalent quality would in the Balearics or on Sardinia's better-known coast, once you account for villa rental split across a group rather than per-room hotel pricing.

I don't hand out enthusiasm easily for places that haven't earned it, and I try not to write reviews that read like marketing. This one holds up under the kind of scrutiny I'd apply to any booking decision — the villa was exactly as described, the island was quieter than its more famous neighbors without pretending to be undiscovered, and the details that usually disappoint on closer inspection simply didn't, here.
