GLYFADA BEACH: OVERLOOKED BY CHOICE, NOT BY CHANCE

 

Glyfada sits on Naxos's southwestern coast, roughly fifteen to sixteen kilometres from Naxos Town, occupying the stretch of shoreline directly between Kastraki to the north and the protected Alyko peninsula to the south. It is, by most measurements, among the longest beaches on the island, running for approximately 2.1 kilometres of fine, nearly white sand, and it owes its enduring quiet less to any real inconvenience of access than to simple visitor habit: with Alyko's better-known cedar forest and coves lying just beyond it, the great majority of passing traffic continues on, leaving Glyfada largely to those who stop deliberately.

A Long, Open Bay of White Sand

The beach forms a wide, open bay of fine sand, pale enough that several regular visitors describe it as nearly white, sloping gently into clear, turquoise water with a smooth, gradual entry from the shoreline. The sea here stays generally calm except when disturbed by wind, and the beach's considerable length means that even during the busiest weeks of the summer season, a short walk from any of its access points is usually enough to find an entirely empty stretch of sand. Large dunes back the beach along much of its length, and stands of cedar trees, in reality a variety of juniper, grow directly out of the sand in places, providing pockets of natural shade uncommon on many of Naxos's more exposed beaches.

A Dry Salt Lake Behind the Dunes

What most clearly distinguishes Glyfada from its neighbours is a substantial salt lake lying just behind the dune line at the beach's southern end. Dry through much of the summer, the lake bed is put to a genuinely unusual practical use each August, when it serves as an informal car park for the beach itself, absorbing the seasonal overflow of visitors without any need for a paved lot. Sand dunes separate the beach proper from the lake bed, and the combination of open water, dry salt flat and cedar-covered dunes gives Glyfada a more varied, layered landscape than a simple strip of sand and sea.

Beneath Oskelos Hill

Immediately behind Glyfada rises Oskelos hill, the same high ground associated with Pyrgos Oskelou, the 17th-century Venetian tower linked to the noble Sommaripa family that gives neighbouring Kastraki its name. From Glyfada's dunes, the hill offers sweeping views back across the full curve of the bay, tying this quieter beach into the same small stretch of Naxian coastline where Venetian defensive architecture, a Mycenaean acropolis and one of the island's most naturally striking landscapes all sit within a few kilometres of one another.

A Renowned Kitesurfing Spot

Glyfada's open bay and consistent coastal winds have made it well known among kitesurfers, who favour its length and relative emptiness over the more crowded, tightly packed conditions found at some of Naxos's busier kite and windsurf beaches further north. The beach's size allows kitesurfers, swimmers and sunbathers to spread out along its length without the sport dominating any single stretch of sand, a balance not every windsurfing beach on the island manages as comfortably.

Deliberately Minimal Infrastructure

Glyfada remains, by design or simply through consistently lower visitor numbers, one of the least developed of Naxos's longer beaches. Facilities directly on the sand are limited to a small number of cafés and basic toilet facilities, with only a handful of dining options in the immediate area, including at least one taverna set directly on the beach itself. A modest range of accommodation, hotels, studios and rooms to rent, operates nearby, though availability is limited enough that early booking is generally advisable for anyone hoping to stay in the immediate area rather than simply visiting for the day. Visitors more accustomed to Naxos's fully organised resort beaches should expect to bring their own shade and equipment here, in keeping with the beach's broader reputation for natural, largely untouched surroundings.

Getting There

Glyfada is reached from Naxos Town via the coastal road running south through Agios Prokopios, Agia Anna, Plaka and Kastraki, with the route paved by asphalt for its entire length. Public buses also serve the beach: line 3 from the central bus station at Naxos Port runs to Glyfada, and visitors arriving by air can pick up a connecting service from the Naxos Camp stop roughly 700 metres from the airport terminal, joining the Plaka–Agia Anna–Chora route that runs approximately every thirty minutes through the summer season. As with much of Naxos's public transport, informing the driver of the intended stop in advance is worthwhile, since not every scheduled stop is made automatically. A rental car nonetheless offers considerably more flexibility for exploring this stretch of coast at a relaxed pace.

Nearby: Between Two More Prominent Neighbours

Glyfada's position between Kastraki and Alyko means it is rarely anyone's sole destination, and is more often visited as part of a longer exploration of Naxos's southwestern coast. Kastraki, immediately to the north, offers its own three-kilometre stretch of undeveloped sand and the historic Oskelos Tower; Alyko, just beyond Glyfada to the south, holds the island's protected cedar forest and a scatter of small, sheltered coves considerably more visited than Glyfada itself. Taken together, the three beaches form one of the most ecologically and visually distinctive stretches of coastline on Naxos, and Glyfada's relative quiet, sandwiched between two better-known neighbours, is very much part of its appeal rather than a shortcoming.

 

Glyfada asks for nothing more than the willingness to drive slightly past Kastraki without stopping at Alyko first, and in return offers over two kilometres of open, near-white sand that rarely needs to be shared. For visitors who have already discovered how much of Naxos's coastline rewards a little extra distance from the nearest car park, Glyfada is very often the missing piece between two more famous beaches on either side of it.
