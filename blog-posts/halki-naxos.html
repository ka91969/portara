Halki, Naxos: The Former Capital of the Tragea Valley

Halki (also written Chalki or Chalkio) lies about 16 kilometres inland from Naxos Town, in the Tragea valley, the largest olive-growing area of the Cyclades. The village was formerly the island’s administrative and commercial centre. Its landmarks include neoclassical mansions, the Byzantine church of Panagia Protothronos, a 17th-century Venetian tower that is still inhabited, and a distillery producing kitron, the island’s citron liqueur, since 1896.

HALKI (CHALKI), NAXOS: THE FORMER CAPITAL OF THE TRAGEA VALLEY

 

Halki is a village of the island’s interior, reached by a drive of some 16 kilometres from Naxos Town through the Tragea valley. Its character differs from the whitewashed coastal settlements of the Cyclades: the centre is made up of ochre and pink neoclassical facades, a Venetian tower with heavy wooden doors and carved coats of arms, a shaded village square, and olive groves on every side. For visitors staying on the island, it is a practical half-day excursion that combines Byzantine art, Venetian architecture and local craft production.

Where Halki Sits, and Why It Mattered

Halki lies at roughly 275 metres above sea level in the middle of the Tragea (Tragaia) valley, the olive-covered basin in the centre of the island that stretches towards Filoti. It is described by the island’s tourism sources as the largest olive-cultivation area of the Cyclades, and the village today serves as the administrative seat of the Drymalia municipal unit. Historically it was more than that: Halki is widely documented as the former capital of Naxos and its main administrative and trading centre, which accounts for the number of substantial mansions in a village of this size.

The wealth is still legible in the architecture. Local heritage sources note that Halki was the first village on the island to receive electricity, an indication of its status in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The houses around the centre show Venetian influence and are often compared to those of Corfu and the Ionian islands rather than to the cubic white villages of the coast.

The origin of the name is not settled. The most common explanation links it to the Greek chalkos (copper or bronze), suggesting a community of bronze-smiths settled here under Venetian rule. Guides present this as speculation rather than documented fact.

The “Mystras of the Aegean”

You will see Halki and the surrounding Tragea valley called the “Mystras of the Aegean,” after the medieval Byzantine fortified town in the Peloponnese. The comparison is about density rather than scale: the valley is dotted with dozens of Byzantine and post-Byzantine churches (guides commonly cite around thirty), several with rare frescoes, inscriptions and sculpture, scattered through the olive groves. Within a few kilometres of the village centre are Agios Georgios Diasoritis, Panagia Drosiani near Moni, Panagia Damiotissa near Kaloxylos, and the churches of Agii Apostoli at Metochi and Agios Ioannis at Kerami. Halki is the most convenient base from which to visit them.

Panagia Protothronos: One of the Oldest Churches on Naxos

Panagia Protothronos (“Our Lady of the First Throne”) stands beside the main road and is the village’s principal monument. Dedicated to the Annunciation, it is generally regarded as one of the oldest churches on the island. Its origins lie in an early Christian basilica, of which the synthronon (the tiered stone seating for clergy) and the bishop’s throne in the apse survive, and the name is usually linked to that surviving episcopal throne. In middle Byzantine times the building was reworked into a transitional cross-in-square church, and an inscription on an architrave records a renovation in 1052. Dates for the earliest phase vary between sources, with figures from the 6th to the 9th century appearing, so no single date for the earliest phase is firmly established.

The interior is its most significant feature. The walls carry five successive layers of decoration, running from the early Christian period through a non-figurative 9th-century phase to paintings of the 10th, 11th and 13th centuries, some of which were detached and replaced once the earliest layer was uncovered, so different eras are now visible side by side. The result is a continuous record of centuries of Byzantine painting in a single building. It remains a working church, so modest dress and quiet behaviour are expected, and if it is closed, enquiring at a nearby café is the usual way to arrange access.

A Walk to Agios Georgios Diasoritis

The church of Agios Georgios Diasoritis lies around half a kilometre from the village centre, reached on foot through olive groves. It is a domed, cross-in-square church usually dated to the 10th–11th century, and it is celebrated for the quality and preservation of its frescoes, many of which are dated between the 11th and 13th centuries. It is regarded as one of the best-preserved Byzantine monuments in the valley. The path is unshaded in places, so water and an early or late-day visit are advisable. Opening arrangements for small rural churches change, so it is worth asking locally before setting out.

The Barozzi-Gratsia Tower

Along the main road stands the Barozzi-Gratsia Tower, also written Grazia or Gratsia, and sometimes associated with the Frangopoulos name as well. It is a three-storey Venetian fortified house built in the 17th century by the Barozzi family, with thick walls, barred windows, heavy wooden doors and the coats of arms of its successive owners. One visitor guide dates its present form to 1742. Towers like this were fortified residences and lookout points in an age of pirate raids, and the island still has a number of them, including the Markopolitis Tower at nearby Akadimi. This one is still inhabited, so it is viewed from the street and is not open for interior visits. For the wider Venetian context, see the guide to the Kastro of Naxos, the fortified town in Chora.

Vallindras and the Story of Kitron

No visit to Halki is complete without the Vallindras Kitron Distillery, tucked into an alley off the village centre. Kitron is the island’s citron liqueur, made with the aromatic leaves of the citron tree rather than the fruit alone, and it comes in three styles that differ in colour and strength: green (sweeter and lighter), yellow, and a clear version that sits in between. Reference sources give the strength as roughly 30–40% ABV depending on the variety. The distillery was officially licensed in 1896 and is still run by the same family, now into its fourth or fifth generation depending on who is counting. Some accounts state that the family was distilling informally before the licence, so the exact origins vary by source; 1896 is the date the distillery itself uses.

The visit is small in scale. There is a modest museum of old copper stills, demijohns, furniture and vintage advertising, and visitors are commonly offered a free tasting of the three varieties. Kitron has a flavour somewhere between orange and lemon: serve it ice-cold after dinner, over crushed ice as an aperitif, or in a cocktail. Export of the liqueur reportedly began in 1928, though little is distributed outside the island today. Opening hours vary by season and listings are not consistent, so check before you plan around it, and go in daytime.

Wandering, Shopping and Lingering

The lanes around the centre hold small craft shops, a weaving workshop, galleries and traditional tavernas with outdoor tables. A typical visit runs as follows: arrive late morning, see the church and the tower, visit the distillery, have lunch in the village, then walk to Agios Georgios Diasoritis in the cooler part of the afternoon. About a kilometre from the centre, the Rachi neighbourhood has more old churches for anyone who wants to keep going.

Practical Tips for Visiting Halki

Halki is about 16 kilometres from Naxos Town, which takes under half an hour by car in normal conditions, and a public bus also serves the village from Chora in roughly half an hour; check the current timetable locally as services change seasonally. The village is easily combined with Filoti, Moni and the mountain road through the valley, which forms a convenient circuit by hired car or private driver. Wear comfortable shoes for the cobbles, carry a shawl or light cover-up for churches, and treat the tower and the surrounding houses as private homes. Late spring and early autumn are generally the most comfortable seasons for walking in the valley.

Quick Answers

Is Halki worth visiting? Halki suits visitors interested in Byzantine art, Venetian architecture and local products, all within one village.

How long do I need? Allow three to four hours for the village, the church, the distillery and lunch, and a little longer if you add the walk to Agios Georgios Diasoritis.

What is Halki famous for? It is best known as the former capital of Naxos, for the Byzantine church of Panagia Protothronos, and for kitron, the island’s citron liqueur.

Is it the same as Chalki or Chalkio? Yes. Halki, Chalki and Chalkio are different transliterations of the same Greek village name.

 

Naxos is best known for its beaches, but its history was shaped as much by its interior. Inland valleys such as Tragea were the island’s agricultural, commercial and religious heartland for centuries, and Halki, as the former capital, preserves much of that record in its churches, towers and mansions.
