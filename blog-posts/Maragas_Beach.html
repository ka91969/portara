MARAGAS BEACH: THE QUIET MIDDLE GROUND OF NAXOS'S WEST COAST

 

Maragas sits on Naxos's western coast, roughly six kilometres from Naxos Town, occupying the stretch of sand directly between Agia Anna to the north and the far longer expanse of Plaka to the south. Geographically, it forms part of the same broad, continuous shoreline that runs unbroken for some four kilometres from Agia Anna's port down to Plaka's southern dunes; as its own named beach, Maragas typically refers to the roughly one-kilometre section beginning a short distance south of the Agia Anna bus stop and running on toward the point where Plaka properly begins. It has earned a reputation, among repeat visitors to the island in particular, as the most naturally shaded, most gently sloping, and in many tellings simply the prettiest section of this entire stretch of coast.

A Beach Defined by What Surrounds It

Maragas's identity is bound up with its position: close enough to Agia Anna's port, tavernas and bus stop to share in that village's convenience, yet far enough along the coast to feel calmer and less built-up. A scenic footpath and boardwalk connects the two beaches directly, making it an easy, level walk from one to the other along the top of the dunes, while a similar route continues south into Plaka. A visitor based at Maragas therefore has, within a short walk in either direction, access to Agia Anna's livelier port-side amenities and Plaka's much longer, wilder open coastline — without needing to commit fully to the character of either.

Fine Sand, Shallow Water and Scattered Shade

The beach itself is composed of fine, soft, pale sand, fronted by clear turquoise water that deepens gradually enough to make Maragas consistently recommended as one of the more family-suitable beaches on this coast, with young children able to wade a considerable distance before the water becomes genuinely deep. Scattered trees along and behind the sand, offering natural shade uncommon on many of the more developed beaches nearby, are frequently cited as one of Maragas's defining features, allowing visitors to choose between organised sunbeds in the open sun or a patch of shaded ground beneath the branches, depending on preference.

One distinctive and less commonly noted seasonal detail sets Maragas apart from its neighbours: in the final weeks of August and into September, wildflowers have been observed growing directly out of the sand itself along parts of the beach, a small but genuinely striking natural phenomenon that late-season visitors occasionally encounter without necessarily expecting it.

Rock Pools, Snorkelling and the Naxos Eye

Rock formations along sections of the Maragas coastline create genuinely rewarding conditions for snorkelling, with a more varied underwater landscape than the open sand further along the coast typically offers. The beach is also known locally for a particular natural curiosity: small, distinctive stones referred to as "Naxos eyes," which can occasionally be found among the rocks and shallows here, both underwater while snorkelling and washed up along the shoreline. These stones hold enough local significance to be worked, on occasion, into simple jewellery such as rings and necklaces by island craftspeople — a small, characterful detail that gives Maragas a point of interest beyond swimming and sunbathing alone.

A Chapel Marking a Gradual Change in Character

A small, whitewashed chapel set on a headland near the southern end of Agia Anna, close to where Maragas begins, functions as an informal landmark for a genuine shift in atmosphere along this coast. North of the chapel, the beach remains firmly within Agia Anna's more organised, family-oriented character; heading south, past the chapel and further into Maragas and onward toward Plaka, the beach grows steadily quieter and less developed, with naturism increasingly common the further south a visitor walks, following the same gradual, unmarked transition that continues all the way into Plaka's own well-established naturist sections.

Amenities, Camping and Somewhere to Stay

Maragas supports a genuine, though comparatively modest, range of visitor infrastructure: sunbeds and umbrellas are available across part of the beach, a water sports centre operates within easy walking distance, and several tavernas and beach bars sit directly on or just behind the sand, offering fresh seafood and casual dining within sight of the water. The area is also home to a well-established beach camping and apartment complex directly on the sand, a genuinely distinctive feature among Naxos's western beaches and one that has, over the years, made Maragas a particular favourite among budget-conscious and long-stay visitors, alongside the area's hotels, studios and villas.

Getting There

Maragas is reached from Naxos Town by car, taxi or regular public bus, with the journey covering roughly six kilometres and taking around fifteen minutes by road. Its position directly beside Agia Anna also makes it easily reachable on foot via the coastal boardwalk for visitors already staying, or spending the day, at the neighbouring beach, and the same walking route continues onward into Plaka for those wanting to extend the walk further south.

Nearby: Completing the Western Coast

Maragas sits squarely within Naxos's longest and most continuously developed run of coastline, with Agia Anna and its small port immediately to the north, Agios Prokopios a short distance further still, and Plaka's four kilometres of dune-backed sand continuing on to the south. Together, the four beaches form a single, more or less unbroken shoreline stretching for the better part of ten kilometres, allowing visitors based anywhere along it to sample every register of Naxos's western coast, from organised family beach to open wilderness, within a single day's walk.

 

Maragas rarely tops any single list of Naxos's most dramatic beaches, and that is, in a real sense, precisely its appeal: a shorter, shadier, gently sloping stretch of coast that asks less of a visitor than its more famous neighbours and quietly rewards those who stop here anyway, whether for the shade, the rock pools, or the small pale stones waiting to be found along the tideline.
