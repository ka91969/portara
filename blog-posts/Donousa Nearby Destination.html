DONOUSA: THE LAST STOP IN THE SMALL CYCLADES

 

Donousa is the easternmost and, by most measures, the least visited of the Small Cyclades, lying sixteen kilometres east of Naxos and some twenty-five kilometres north of Amorgos. It covers just under fourteen square kilometres, rises to 385 metres at the summit of Mount Papas, and is home to only around 213 permanent residents according to the most recent census, most of them living in a single combined port-and-village that breaks with the usual Cycladic pattern of a separate harbour and hilltop chora. Of the four islands in this blog's coverage of the Small Cyclades, Donousa is the one that asks the most of a visitor to actually reach, and rewards that effort with the least developed landscape of the group.

Where Dionysus Hid Ariadne a Second Time

Donousa's own mythology extends directly from the story already covered in this blog's guide to the Portara on Naxos: according to local tradition, it was here that the god Dionysus hid Ariadne, the Cretan princess abandoned by Theseus on the Naxian shore, to keep her safe from being found again. Whether read as a genuine variant tradition or simply a later elaboration tying a small, obscure island into Naxos's own defining myth, the story gives Donousa a mythological pedigree considerably larger than its size would otherwise suggest, and a further, little-known thread in the same Ariadne-and-Dionysus story that runs through so much of this blog's coverage of Naxos itself.

Getting There: The Furthest Reach of the Ferry Network

Donousa has no airport, and every visit begins with a boat. The most practical route from a Naxos base is a direct ferry connection, typically taking between one and two hours depending on the vessel, considerably more manageable than the alternative of routing via Piraeus with multiple intermediate stops. Sailings are noticeably less frequent than to Koufonisia, Iraklia or Schinoussa, all covered elsewhere on this blog, and drop further still outside the summer months, making it essential to check the current timetable and build genuine slack into any itinerary rather than assuming a convenient same-day connection will be available.

Stavros and the Island's Three Hamlets

Stavros, also known simply as Donousa, serves as both the island's port and its main village at once, an amphitheatre of whitewashed houses rising directly from the harbour rather than the more usual Cycladic arrangement of a coastal port feeding a separate, defensively sited hilltop town. Three smaller hamlets complete the island's settled population: Mersini, on the southeastern coast, overlooked by the hilltop chapel of Agia Sofia; Kalotaritissa, near the northern coastline, holding a single taverna and a scatter of small beaches; and Charavgi, in the south. Only eleven kilometres of paved road connect these settlements; everything beyond that short network is reached on foot.

Exile, Piracy and a Mine That Closed in 1938

Donousa has been inhabited since prehistory, and excavation on a headland above the sea has revealed the remains of a Geometric-period settlement, the island's most prosperous era, reaching a genuine peak around the ninth century BC when its position on the sea route linking mainland Greece, the Dodecanese and the coast of Asia Minor made it a useful anchorage for passing ships. Later history was considerably harder: Rome used the island as a place of exile, pirates sheltered here through the Middle Ages in the same pattern that shaped so much of the wider Cyclades, and a small mining operation, worked into the twentieth century, closed only in 1938, one more layer in an island whose quiet present sits atop a genuinely eventful past.

A New Addition to Greece's National Walking Network

What Donousa lacks in roads it makes up for in footpaths. In 2025, the island was formally added to the Greek Islands Trail, the country's national network of marked island walking routes, recognition of a hiking infrastructure locals had already relied on for generations. The flagship route runs from Stavros to Kalotaritissa, crossing the shoulder of Mount Papas through vineyards, olive groves and the visible remains of the island's old mine workings, a genuinely rewarding full-day walk that takes in a considerable cross-section of Donousa's history and landscape in a single route. The island's terrain itself splits into two distinct characters: jagged, dramatic cliffs and sea caves along the northeast coast, giving way to a softer, more sheltered southern shore where a string of sandy bays holds some of the clearest water anywhere in this stretch of the Aegean.

Kedros, Livadi and a Fishing Boat Called the Sorceress

Only the beach at Stavros itself is formally organised; everything else on Donousa is reached on foot or by boat. Kedros, generally considered the island's finest beach, lies a short downhill walk from a coastal path above it, reachable in around ten minutes once the main uphill stretch from Stavros is complete; the wreck of a small vessel, the Orion, rests visibly in the shallows here, an unplanned but genuinely striking addition to the view. Livadi, further along the coast and more isolated still, is usually reached by small boat rather than on foot. A well-loved local option is to charter the fishing boat Donousa Magissa, "Sorceress Donousa," out of Stavros: a typical circuit calls at Kedros, continues to Livadi, passes the beach of Fykio, stops for snorkelling at the sea cave of Fokospilia, home to Mediterranean monk seals, and finishes at the remote hamlet of Kalotaritissa and its own scatter of small beaches. A second notable cave, Spilia Tichou, the "Cave of the Wall," adds a further stop for visitors particularly drawn to the island's coastal geology.

Small Churches and a Handful of Village Festivals

Donousa's traditional Cycladic churches punctuate the island's landscape and its calendar in roughly equal measure. The Church of Timios Stavros, the Holy Cross, in the main village, celebrates its feast on September 14th; the hilltop chapel of Agia Sofia outside Mersini holds its own celebration on September 17th; and the church of Panagia-Ai Giannis celebrates on August 15th, in keeping with the wider Greek observance of the Dormition of the Virgin. Each remains a genuinely local occasion rather than a visitor spectacle, offering travellers whose stay happens to coincide with one a rare, unstaged glimpse of the island's own rhythms.

No Souvenir Shops, and Nothing Trying to Impress You

Donousa's small handful of family-run guesthouses, several offering sunset views toward Naxos, Amorgos, Koufonisia and the uninhabited Makares islets, and its equally small run of tavernas in Stavros, define the practical limits of what the island offers by way of infrastructure, and there is, deliberately or otherwise, no souvenir trade to speak of. What visitors tend to come away with instead is harder to put a price on: a taverna owner's name still remembered months later, salt still in your hair from an afternoon at Livadi, and a fairly strong suspicion that the best small islands in the Aegean are generally the ones that never tried especially hard to be found.

Planning the Visit

Given the ferry schedule and the genuinely limited accommodation on the island, Donousa is best treated as an overnight or multi-night stay rather than a rushed day trip from Naxos, and booking well ahead of a July or August visit is essential given how few rooms the island actually has. Visitors planning a longer Small Cyclades itinerary will find Donousa combines naturally with Koufonisia, Iraklia and Schinoussa, all covered elsewhere on this blog, via the same local ferry network, though the sparser sailing schedule to Donousa specifically means it is worth building in extra flexibility at both ends of the trip.

Nearby: Completing the Small Cyclades

Donousa marks the natural final stop in this blog's coverage of the Small Cyclades, following Koufonisia, Iraklia and Schinoussa, and together the four islands offer visitors based on Naxos a genuinely complete picture of just how much variation exists within a single small archipelago: fashionable and growing at Koufonisia, remote and cave-riddled at Iraklia, softly pastoral and quietly exclusive at Schinoussa, and, at Donousa, further still, wilder still, and, for now, still genuinely uncrowded.

 

Most islands this small eventually get discovered properly, a ferry route upgraded, a boutique hotel opens, and the rest follows. Donousa has had every opportunity to go the same way and has, so far, simply declined. Eleven kilometres of road, a walking trail added to the national network only last year, a fishing boat named for a sorceress, and a myth borrowed from its much larger neighbour: for visitors who have already explored what Naxos itself has to offer, this is very often the furthest, and the quietest, point worth reaching.
