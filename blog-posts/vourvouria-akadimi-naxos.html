Vourvouria and Akadimi, Naxos: Two Small Villages of the Tragea Valley

Vourvouria and Akadimi are two small settlements in the central Tragea valley, each within a short distance of Halki. Vourvouria is a nearly emptied stone village between two streams, with several small churches; Akadimi is known for the Markopolitis tower house of 1776, a rare tower built by a local Orthodox family rather than by Venetian lords. Both are best seen on foot as part of a walk through the valley.

VOURVOURIA AND AKADIMI, NAXOS: TWO SMALL VILLAGES OF THE TRAGEA VALLEY

 

Between Halki and Damarionas, the Tragea valley holds a number of settlements that are too small to appear on most itineraries. Vourvouria and Akadimi are two of them. Neither has a museum or a visitor centre, but each has a historic landmark that explains something about the island: a village that peaked in the 19th and early 20th centuries and has since emptied, and a fortified house that records a local challenge to feudal landlords in the late 18th century.

Location and Setting

Both villages lie in central Naxos, within the Drymalia area. Vourvouria sits in a fertile area between two small streams, on the road that links Halki, Damarionas and Filoti; it is about 700 metres from Damarionas. Akadimi (also written Akadimoi) is in the heart of the Tragea valley, with its tower on the side of the village that borders Halki; one reference gives the elevation of the tower as about 290 metres. The surrounding landscape is the same in both cases: olive groves, small fields and old paths, within the valley widely known as the island’s principal olive-growing area.

Vourvouria

Vourvouria is a small stone village. According to one version, the name comes from vourla, a word for the reeds that grow along the streams. The oldest written record of the village dates to 1538, and it is mentioned by the French explorer Joseph Pitton de Tournefort, who visited Naxos around the turn of the 18th century, as well as in the work of the Venetian cartographer Vincenzo Coronelli, whose reference to a place called “Vurecuria” historians identify with Vourvouria.

Population figures show a sharp decline. One local source reports that until 1950 the village had more than 150 permanent residents and produced significant quantities of olive oil, whereas at the time of its publication no more than about 25 people lived there year-round and many houses were abandoned. Current numbers are likely to differ and are not formally published here. The village architecture is described as uniform, with stone houses featuring large balconies (pountia), arches (volta) and courtyards; a popular story says the whole village was built by a single craftsman, which is anecdotal.

The village has several small churches. The parish church of Agios Prokopios dates from around 1809 to 1810, and its feast on 8 July includes a procession of icons through the lanes to the sound of bells, according to local custom. The post-Byzantine church of Agios Georgios is dated to 1762 and has been restored. One guide also lists the churches of Agia Paraskevi (given as 1397) and Agios Konstantinos (given as 1311); these early dates come from a single source and should be treated as approximate.

Akadimi

Akadimi is described as a compact village of small, closely set houses with gardens, in the middle of the Tragea valley. Its landmark is the Markopolitis tower, also called the Markopolitis-Papadakis tower, which stands at the entrance to the village on the side facing Halki and is close to the Barozzi tower at Halki.

The tower is dated to 1776 by an inscription on the lintel of the second-floor entrance, which also names Markos Politis, below a coat of arms. It has three levels, strong walls and, according to one description, a drawbridge, and is regarded as a bastion of local resistance. Most towers on Naxos were built by Venetian or Catholic noble families, and the Markopolitis tower is one of the few built by a local Orthodox owner. Markos Politis is described as a leader of a revolt against feudal landowners in the Tragea; he was exiled to Lesvos and hanged by the Ottoman authorities in 1802. The tower has belonged to the Papadakis family since 1888. Its condition is described as relatively good, and because it is a private property, it is generally viewed from the road and access to the interior should not be assumed.

Other points of interest in Akadimi include the watermills of Barozzi and Goufas, a traditional olive mill and the Byzantine church of the Theotokos (Panagia), the patron saint of the village, whose feast is on 21 November. The route-planning page of the island’s tourism office also lists a church of Agios Nikolaos at Akadimi, dated to the 11th century, on its Byzantine churches trail.

Walking Between the Villages

Both villages sit on routes through the Tragea. A circular trail published by the island’s tourism authorities starts at Halki, passes Agios Georgios Diasoritis and the churches around Monitsia (Rachi), continues through Moni and Kaloxylos, and returns through Akadimi, passing the Panagia Damiotissa church near Kaloxylos and the Markopolitis tower on the way. A shorter option links Halki and Filoti, with the tower and the church of Panagia Akadimiotissa on either side of the path. Paths can be rough and unshaded, and signage varies, so water and a map or downloaded route are advisable.

Visiting Vourvouria and Akadimi

Both villages are on or near the roads between Naxos Town, Halki and Filoti, roughly 16 kilometres from Naxos Town, and are easily reached by car or taxi within a short drive of Halki. Public buses on the inland route serve the main road, but timetables are seasonal and should be checked at the terminal. There are few or no services in either village, and visitors generally eat and shop in Halki or Filoti (see the Halki guide). Both can be combined with Damarionas and Damalas (Damarionas guide, Damalas guide) and with Moni and Panagia Drosiani (Moni guide). Because these are residential villages, visitors should keep to public lanes and treat churches as places of worship; small churches are often locked outside service times. Late spring and early autumn are generally the more comfortable seasons for walking.

Quick Answers

What is the Markopolitis tower? It is a three-level tower house at Akadimi, dated 1776 by an inscription, built by the local Orthodox leader Markos Politis and owned by the Papadakis family since 1888.

Who was Markos Politis? A local leader of a revolt against feudal landowners in the Tragea, who was exiled to Lesvos and hanged by the Ottoman authorities in 1802.

How many people live in Vourvouria? Very few. One local source reported no more than about 25 permanent residents, compared with over 150 until 1950; current figures may differ.

Can the tower be visited? It is private property, so it is normally viewed from outside; any interior access should be confirmed locally.

 

Vourvouria and Akadimi show two different outcomes within the same valley: one village shrunk by 20th-century emigration but still marked by its churches and stone houses, the other defined by a single fortified house that records a local political history.
