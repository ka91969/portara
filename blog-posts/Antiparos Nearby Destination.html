Antiparos: The Cyclades' Quietest Address

A ten-minute ferry beyond Paros, across a channel barely a nautical mile wide, sits an island that has spent six centuries mastering the art of being deliberately overlooked. Antiparos has a 15th-century Venetian castle, the only vertical show cave in Europe, and an ancient sanctuary still being excavated on an islet just offshore — and, more recently, a reputation as the one place in the Cyclades where genuinely famous people can disappear entirely.

ANTIPAROS: THE CYCLADES' QUIETEST ADDRESS

 

Antiparos sits less than a nautical mile off the western coast of Paros itself already covered elsewhere on this blog as the natural first excursion from Naxos, separated from it by a narrow channel that makes the two islands feel, on arrival, almost like a single extended coastline rather than two distinct destinations. Reaching Antiparos always means passing through Paros first; there is no direct connection from Naxos or anywhere else. For travellers willing to add that second short crossing to an already-planned Paros day, what waits on the other side is one of the more understated and, in recent years, most quietly sought-after small islands anywhere in the Cyclades.

From Oliaros to "Opposite Paros"

The island's ancient name was Oliaros, a word most likely of Phoenician origin meaning "wooded mountain," under which it was known to classical geographers including Strabo. Its modern name is considerably more literal: Antiparos simply means "opposite Paros," a description of its geography rather than any deeper etymology, adopted once the island's identity became defined almost entirely by its relationship to its larger neighbour.

Getting There: Always Through Paros

Antiparos is reached exclusively via Paros, and the crossing itself could hardly be more straightforward. A vehicle ferry runs from the small port of Pounda, on Paros's west coast, roughly every thirty minutes throughout the day, covering the short strait in seven to ten minutes at minimal cost. A separate passenger ferry also connects Antiparos Town directly to Parikia, Paros's main port, taking closer to thirty minutes. For visitors approaching from Naxos, the most practical plan is to treat Antiparos as a considered extension of a Paros day rather than a separate undertaking: an early ferry from Naxos to Parikia, a transfer across to Pounda, and a short hop over the strait, all comfortably achievable well before midday.

A Venetian Castle With Naxian Bloodlines

At the centre of Antiparos Town, known locally as Chora, stands a fortified Venetian castle built in 1440 by Giovanni Loredan, a nobleman of Venice's patrician Loredan family. At the time, the island had become little more than a base for pirates; Loredan brought new inhabitants to Antiparos at his own expense and built the castle as a single fortified residence, now recognised as an unusually well-preserved example of the kind of defended settlement built across the Cyclades throughout the Latin period of Venetian rule. The connections run deeper still for anyone already familiar with Naxos's own Venetian history, covered extensively elsewhere on this blog: Loredan married Maria Sommaripa, a member of the same noble Sommaripa family whose coat of arms appears on towers and mansions across Naxos, and by 1480 the castle and island had passed, through marriage, to Domenico Pisani, son of the Duke of Crete, whose own wife was the daughter of the Duke of Naxos himself. Antiparos, in other words, was never a peripheral footnote to the Duchy of the Archipelago; it was bound into the same small network of Venetian noble families that built the Kastro of Naxos Town and its fortified towers.

Today, the castle forms the historic heart of a genuinely charming, low-key town: a sheltered harbour looking out toward Despotiko, cobbled lanes threading between traditional houses, and a small central square that retains, despite decades of steady tourism, the unhurried feel of a proper island settlement rather than a resort built for visitors alone.

Europe's Only Vertical Show Cave

Antiparos's single best-known attraction lies eight kilometres from Chora: the Cave of Antiparos, also known as the Cave of Agios Ioannis, widely described as the only vertical show cave anywhere in Europe, descending to a depth of roughly eighty-five metres across several distinct levels. Its centrepiece, a colossal stalagmite known as the Huge Central Column, is estimated at some forty-five million years old, making it among the oldest such formations on the continent. The cave has drawn visitors for millennia rather than mere centuries: ancient inscriptions on its walls are traditionally associated with the lyric poet Archilochus of Paros, and later visitors included King Otto of Greece, whose visit gave the cave's Royal Chamber its name, and, in 1673, the Marquis de Nointel, French ambassador to the Ottoman court, who was so struck by the cave's central formation that he celebrated Christmas Mass inside it, before his party proceeded to break off pieces of the surrounding stalactites and stalagmites as souvenirs, the first of several recorded episodes of damage to the site over the centuries that followed. A small 18th-century chapel, Agios Ioannis Spiliotis, stands at the cave's entrance and hosts a local festival each May; the cave itself, damaged further during the German occupation of Greece, underwent a full renovation with European Union funding in the late 2000s, adding safe modern stairs and railings without compromising the raw scale of the descent. It remains open daily through the main season, with the final entry generally around half an hour before closing.

Despotiko: An Ancient Sanctuary Still Being Uncovered

A short boat ride from Antiparos's southern coast, typically departing from the settlement of Agios Georgios, leads to Despotiko, a small, uninhabited islet identified by both the ancient geographer Strabo and the Roman writer Pliny the Elder as Prepesinthos. Despotiko holds one of the most significant, and most actively ongoing, archaeological excavations anywhere in the Cyclades: the Sanctuary of Mandra, a major religious sanctuary founded in the seventh century BC and remaining in active cult use through the Roman period. Excavations, still very much underway, have recovered swords, jewellery and small statues from the site, alongside substantial restoration work on the sanctuary's standing architecture. For visitors already familiar with Naxos's own extensive Early Iron Age and Archaic archaeology, covered at length elsewhere on this blog through sites including Yria and the Temple of Demeter, Despotiko offers a genuinely direct point of comparison: a Cycladic sanctuary of comparable date and importance, still, quite literally, being pulled from the ground. Boats from Antiparos typically combine the crossing with time on Despotiko's own beach, Livadi, a clean, quiet stretch of sand well suited to finishing the visit with a swim.

From Bohemian Retreat to Discreet Getaway

Antiparos holds a genuine claim to having been one of Greece's original "alternative" travel destinations, drawing artists, writers and independent travellers well before mainstream Cycladic tourism discovered it. That reputation has, in recent years, evolved rather than disappeared: the island is now understood as one of the more discreet, low-key retreats favoured by a number of well-known figures seeking privacy rather than visibility, most publicly the actor Tom Hanks and his wife Rita Wilson, who own a home on the island, alongside several prominent Greek shipping families with properties there. The result is an island that manages a genuinely unusual combination: a bohemian, unpretentious surface character, paired with a level of understated exclusivity that has made it, for a certain kind of traveller, the preferred quiet alternative to Paros's increasingly polished harbour towns.

Beaches and a Flat, Cycling-Friendly Landscape

Antiparos's coastline offers a good range of beaches within easy reach of Chora, including Psaralyki, Sifneiko, Agios Spyridon and a scatter of quieter coves further south toward Soros and Glyfa. The island's coastline is also known for a number of dramatic sea caves, best appreciated from the water on one of the boat tours that circle the island from Antiparos Town, and, for anyone flying into Paros rather than arriving by sea, visible from the air on the left-hand side of the aircraft during approach. Antiparos itself is small and largely flat, making a rented bicycle a genuinely practical way to get around for visitors not travelling with a car, and buses run to the more popular beaches during the summer season for those who prefer not to cycle.

Planning the Visit

Antiparos is best treated as an addition to a Paros day rather than a wholly separate excursion from Naxos, given the need to pass through Paros regardless of the route taken. A well-organised day allows time for the castle and old town in Chora, a visit to the cave, and, with an earlier start, a short crossing to Despotiko as well, though fitting all three into a single day from a Naxos base is ambitious rather than impossible. Travellers drawn specifically to Antiparos's quieter character, or hoping to experience its evening atmosphere once the day-trip crowds have returned to Paros, are better served by an overnight stay, for which accommodation should be booked well in advance during the peak summer months, when the island's genuine popularity among repeat visitors tends to outstrip its comparatively limited supply of rooms.

Nearby: Completing the Central Cyclades

Antiparos pairs naturally with Paros itself, covered in detail elsewhere on this blog, and the two islands are, in practical terms, best planned as a single combined excursion from a Naxos base rather than two separate trips. The nearby islet of Saliagos, considered the most ancient known settlement anywhere in the Cyclades, adds a further, deeper layer of prehistory to the same small stretch of water, while the Small Cyclades to the southeast offer a natural next step for travellers building out a longer island-hopping itinerary around Naxos.

 

Most small islands in the Cyclades have had to choose, at some point, between staying quiet and becoming fashionable. Antiparos has spent six hundred years managing to do both at once: a fortified Venetian town built by marriage into the same noble network that shaped Naxos itself, a cave visited by kings and ambassadors, a sanctuary still giving up its secrets on the islet across the water, and, through it all, an island that has never quite let any of that get in the way of feeling like somewhere worth keeping to yourself.
