Plaka Beach: Naxos's Longest Stretch of Sand

South of Agia Anna, the coastline stops being a beach in the conventional sense and becomes something closer to a landscape: four uninterrupted kilometres of dune-backed sand, tapering from organised sunbeds in the north to open, windblown wilderness in the south. Plaka is the longest continuous beach on Naxos, and one shaped as much by its free-spirited history as by its geography.

PLAKA BEACH: NAXOS'S LONGEST STRETCH OF SAND

 

Plaka sits on the southwestern coast of Naxos, roughly eight kilometres from Naxos Town, forming a direct natural continuation of Agia Anna to its north and running on toward Mikri Vigla to the south. At approximately four kilometres in length, it is the longest continuous beach on the island by a considerable margin, and among the longest anywhere in the Cyclades — a scale that allows it to function, within a single stretch of coastline, as several quite different beaches at once, from a fully organised family resort at its northern end to open, near-empty dune country toward the south.

From Hippie Hideaway to Island Favourite

Plaka's modern identity was shaped well before conventional tourism development reached this side of Naxos. Through the 1960s and 1970s, the beach became a known gathering point for hippies, backpackers and other free spirits drawn to the Cyclades during that era, a period during which the beach earned an enduring reputation as a nudist-friendly stretch of coast. As the wider island's tourism economy grew in subsequent decades, Plaka developed into one of Naxos's most popular beaches overall, visited today by families, couples and independent travellers of every kind — yet it has retained a genuine trace of that original free-spirited character, particularly along its quieter southern reaches, in a way few other heavily visited Greek beaches manage to preserve.

Dunes, Tamarisk and a Landscape in Transition

The beach itself is a long, wide expanse of fine, pale golden sand, backed for much of its length by a system of low, undulating dunes rather than any hard promenade or built embankment. Rows of feathery tamarisk trees, locally sometimes referred to as cedars, grow along sections of the dune line, providing patches of natural shade increasingly rare on the island's more developed beaches. Flat rocks interrupt the otherwise sandy shoreline at certain points, and sparse coastal vegetation threads through the dunes further back from the water, giving Plaka a distinctly wilder, more naturalistic character than the more manicured beaches immediately to its north.

That character intensifies steadily the further south a visitor walks. The northern kilometre or so, closest to Agia Anna, is Plaka's most developed section, lined with sunbeds, umbrellas, beach bars and tavernas built just behind the dune line. Moving south, the density of facilities thins progressively, the crowds fall away, and the beach gradually opens into long, largely empty stretches of sand where the only sounds are wind through the dune grass and the sea itself — a transition local guides often describe as crossing into a different, more rustic world within the same beach.

A Long-Standing Naturist Tradition

Plaka's association with naturism, dating to its 1960s and 1970s origins, remains genuinely active today, concentrated mainly toward the beach's southern reaches, where clothing-optional bathing has long been an accepted norm rather than a fringe practice. There is no fixed, formally marked boundary between the beach's more conventional northern stretch and its naturist southern sections; the atmosphere simply grows quieter and more relaxed with distance, and the exact position of any informal dividing line has been known to shift somewhat from season to season and even day to day. Visitors uncomfortable with this arrangement are best served sticking to the organised northern kilometre, while those seeking Plaka's original character will find it, largely undisturbed, toward the far south.

Three Brothers and the Beach's Working Landmarks

Along Plaka's length, a handful of long-established tavernas function as informal waypoints for both locals and regular visitors, none more well known than the Three Brothers taverna, a fixture of the beach for decades and a commonly used point of reference when giving directions along the otherwise unbroken sand. Tavernas of this kind, tucked just behind the dunes, serve fresh seafood and traditional Naxian dishes within sight, and often within earshot, of the water — a low-key dining tradition that has developed alongside, rather than displaced, Plaka's more untouched southern character.

West-Facing Sunsets Over an Open Horizon

Facing directly west across the open Aegean, Plaka catches wide, largely unobstructed sunsets on all but the haziest evenings, the low dunes and sparse vegetation doing nothing to interrupt the view. With considerably more open horizon than the more built-up beaches further north, Plaka's evening light is frequently cited among the more atmospheric sunset settings on this coast, best appreciated from one of the beachfront tavernas or from a quiet spot among the dunes themselves.

Amenities and Accommodation

Despite its wilder reputation, Plaka is considered one of the better-equipped beaches on Naxos in terms of visitor infrastructure, at least along its organised northern stretch: restaurants, tavernas, mini markets, bars and cafés operate behind the dune line, water sports facilities are available at several points along the shore, and a substantial range of accommodation, from hotels to studios, apartments and private rooms, serves the surrounding area. Sunbeds and umbrellas can be rented across much of the beach's developed section, while the southern reaches remain, by design, considerably more sparse.

Getting There

Plaka is most directly reached by following the coastal route from Naxos Town through Agios Prokopios and Agia Anna: asphalt as far as Agia Anna, continuing afterward along a coastal dirt road generally kept in good condition. Several longer inland alternatives also connect Naxos Town to Plaka, running via the villages of Agios Arsenios, Glinado and Vivlos, each covering a somewhat greater distance than the direct coastal road. Regular bus services also run from Naxos Town, making the beach accessible without a rental car, though a vehicle offers considerably more flexibility for exploring the beach's full four-kilometre length and the quieter beaches further south.

Nearby: The Southwestern Coast Continues

Plaka forms the middle link of Naxos's long southwestern beach corridor, with Agia Anna and Agios Prokopios lying to the north and Mikri Vigla, along with the quieter Aliko peninsula and its own naturist coves, continuing the coastline further south. Together, this stretch represents the single longest continuous run of sandy, swimmable coastline on the island, and Plaka's considerable length makes it a natural, flexible midpoint for visitors planning to sample several of these beaches within a single day.

 

Few beaches anywhere in the Cyclades offer quite this range within a single, unbroken shoreline: organised sunbeds and taverna lunches at one end, and four kilometres later, open dunes, tamarisk shade and a genuine echo of the free-spirited coast this beach was fifty years ago. Plaka rewards exactly the kind of visitor willing to keep walking past the crowd — the beach only gets quieter, and in most tellings, better, the further south you go.
