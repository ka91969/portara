Mikri Vigla: One Headland, Two Completely Different Beaches

A single rocky cape, once a manned lookout post for pirate raids, now divides Naxos's most famous stretch of coast into two beaches that could hardly feel more different: on one side, some of the most reliable kitesurfing wind in Europe; on the other, a sheltered, near-windless bay perfect for a slow swim.

MIKRI VIGLA: ONE HEADLAND, TWO COMPLETELY DIFFERENT BEACHES

 

Mikri Vigla sits on Naxos's western coast, roughly twelve kilometres from Naxos Town, between Plaka and Orkos to the north and Kastraki to the south. Geographically, it is defined by a single small headland jutting out into the Aegean, splitting the coastline into two beaches so different in character that many visitors are surprised to learn they share a name at all. One side draws windsurfers and kitesurfers from across Europe; the other, sheltered entirely from the wind that makes its neighbour famous, is among the calmest, most family-friendly stretches of sand on the island.

A Name Left Over from the Age of Pirates

The name Mikri Vigla translates directly from Greek as "small watchtower," from mikri, meaning small, and vigla, an ancient Greek term for a lookout or watch post. It refers to an observation point that once stood on top of the cape, manned during the Frankish and Venetian periods to provide advance warning of pirate ships approaching the island — the same threat that, elsewhere on Naxos, drove the construction of fortified towers like Bazeos and Oskelos, and shaped the design of Apano Kastro. The watchman stationed here, known in Greek as a viglatoras, would light a signal fire on spotting an approaching vessel, alerting Naxos Town and the surrounding villages in time to prepare a response. No structure from that original watch post survives today, but the cape's commanding view up and down the coastline still makes the historical logic of the site immediately obvious to anyone standing on it.

Parthena: A World-Class Wind Beach

On the northern side of the headland lies Parthena, named for the small, uninhabited islet of the same name rising just offshore. This beach runs for roughly 600 metres of soft, golden sand, fronting deep blue water that is regularly disturbed, in the most productive sense, by the meltemi, the strong, dry northerly wind that blows across the Cyclades from May to September. Local geography intensifies that wind further still: the cape's shape creates a Venturi effect, accelerating north-northwesterly winds and frequently delivering conditions five to ten knots stronger than general regional forecasts predict. That combination of reliability and strength has made Parthena one of the most internationally recognised windsurfing and kitesurfing locations in Europe, drawing dedicated wind-sport travellers specifically for its conditions rather than as an incidental stop on a wider island tour. Several fully equipped sports centres operate directly on the beach, offering rental equipment, supervised lessons for beginners and storage and support for more experienced riders, and the sight of dozens of kites moving in formation against the silhouette of the Parthena islet has become one of the more recognisable images associated with this stretch of Naxos's coast.

Sahara and Limanaki: Shelter on the Other Side

Cross the dunes behind the headland, a walk of only a few hundred metres, and the wind drops away almost entirely. The southern beach, known locally as Sahara or Limanaki, extends for a considerable distance — among the longer beaches in the Cyclades by some measurements — its shallow, gradually deepening water and crystal clarity making it particularly well suited to families and to swimmers uninterested in fighting a headwind. The irregular, wind-carved rocks of the Mikri Vigla cape curve around part of this southern shore, forming a natural, sheltered harbour once used by small local boats, which gives the "Limanaki," or "little harbour," name its origin. Just a few metres inland from this stretch of coast lies a small lake that was historically used for salt production, one of Naxos's traditional export goods in earlier centuries — a modest but genuine link between this beach and the island's older agricultural economy, in the same way the salt lake behind Agios Prokopios and the dry lake bed at Glyfada point to a similar, once-widespread local industry along this coast.

A Beach That Has Largely Escaped Mass Development

Despite its considerable international reputation among wind-sport travellers, Mikri Vigla has notably avoided the large-scale concrete resort development seen in comparably famous kitesurfing destinations elsewhere in the Mediterranean. Accommodation in the surrounding area tends toward smaller hotels, studios and rooms rather than sprawling complexes, and a number of modestly priced tavernas serve good local food, several with genuine sea views, without the beach ever feeling overtaken by its own popularity. Basic amenities, restrooms and showers, are available, though visitors are generally advised to bring their own sunbeds and umbrellas during the busiest weeks of the season, when rental stock can run short.

Beyond Wind Sports

While windsurfing and kitesurfing remain Mikri Vigla's best-known draw, the surrounding area supports a genuinely wide range of other activities, including swimming, snorkelling, scuba diving, hiking and cycling across the low hills and dune trails behind both beaches. Visitors with no interest in wind sports at all are well served simply by choosing which side of the headland suits the day's conditions and their own preference for either an energetic, sport-focused beach or a calmer, more traditional one.

Getting There

Mikri Vigla is reached from Naxos Town via the coastal road running south through Agios Prokopios, Agia Anna and Plaka, a drive of around twelve kilometres taking roughly twenty minutes, with the route offering scenic views across this stretch of coastline for much of the way. The beach is easily accessible by car or taxi, and regular public buses also serve the area from Naxos Town's central station.

Nearby: The Heart of the Southwestern Coast

Mikri Vigla sits at the midpoint of Naxos's long run of southwestern beaches, within easy reach of Orkos and Plaka to the north and Kastraki, Glyfada and Alyko continuing the coastline to the south, all covered elsewhere on this blog. Its position, genuinely close to virtually every other beach along this stretch, makes Mikri Vigla a natural base for visitors planning to explore the full length of Naxos's southwestern shore over several days.

 

Few single stretches of coast anywhere in the Cyclades offer quite this range within a short walk: a beach built almost entirely around wind, and a beach defined by its absence, separated by a rocky cape that once existed for the sole purpose of watching the horizon for danger. Whether the draw is a kite line or a quiet swim, Mikri Vigla generally has the right side of the headland waiting.
