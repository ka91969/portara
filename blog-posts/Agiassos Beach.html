Agiassos: The Bay Where the Duchy of Naxos Began

Eight hundred years before it became the quietest beach on the island's southwestern coast, this small, wind-sheltered bay was where a Venetian nobleman's ships first came ashore in 1207 — the opening move in a conquest that would reshape Naxos for the next three and a half centuries. Today it is simply the last beach on the road, and, most days, the emptiest.

AGIASSOS: THE BAY WHERE THE DUCHY OF NAXOS BEGAN

 

Agiassos sits at the southwestern tip of Naxos, roughly twenty-one to twenty-three kilometres from Naxos Town depending on the route taken, four kilometres beyond Pyrgaki and five beyond Alyko. It is, by most accounts, the last beach on this side of the island reachable by car, and one of its quietest: a modest, sheltered bay of fine golden sand that sees a fraction of the visitors drawn to the busier beaches closer to town. Its present-day tranquility sits, somewhat improbably, on top of one of the more consequential moments in the island's entire history.

Where Marco Sanudo Came Ashore

According to the historical record, it was in the bay at Agiassos that the Venetian nobleman Marco Sanudo first landed on Naxos in 1207, in the opening move of the campaign that would end with him founding the Duchy of the Archipelago and installing himself as its first duke. From this beach, Sanudo marched inland to besiege Apaliros, the fortified Byzantine-era capital covered elsewhere in this series' article on Apano Kastro, where he met genuine and sustained resistance before finally taking the fortress and beginning the process that reshaped Naxos, and much of the central Aegean, for the following three and a half centuries. It is a striking thought to hold on an otherwise unremarkable stretch of sand: the entire Venetian, and later partly Ottoman, history of Naxos, its Kastro, its noble families, its fortified towers and monasteries, traces its opening chapter back to this specific bay.

The Valley That Still Carries the Name

That early significance left a lasting mark on the map itself. The inland route connecting the village of Sagri to the coast at Agiassos is still known locally as the Agiassos valley, and it is this same valley, roughly eight kilometres of gradual downhill road, that passes directly by Bazeos Tower, the fortified 17th-century monastery of Timios Stavros covered in detail elsewhere on this blog. Travelling from Sagri to Agiassos beach today means descending through the same landscape Sanudo's forces would have moved through eight centuries earlier, past a monument built four centuries after his landing but standing, in its own way, as a later chapter in the same defensive, contested history that began at this coastline.

A Small, Sheltered Bay

The beach itself extends for roughly 750 to 800 metres of fine, soft, golden sand, fronting shallow, crystal-clear water that deepens gradually enough to make Agiassos a consistently recommended choice for families with young children. What distinguishes the bay most clearly is its shelter: surrounding hills protect it from the meltemi and the northern winds that regularly disturb more exposed beaches elsewhere on the island, leaving the water at Agiassos calm even on days when much of the rest of Naxos's coastline is considerably rougher. That same clarity and calm has also made the bay a genuinely good spot for diving, with small coves and gorges nearby offering sheltered underwater terrain for those exploring beneath the surface.

A Wetland at One End

At one end of the beach lies a small wetland, home to a range of bird species that make use of the sheltered habitat it provides — a modest ecological footnote consistent with the pattern of small coastal wetlands found at several points along this stretch of Naxos's shoreline, echoing the salt lake behind Agios Prokopios and the seasonal lake at Glyfada further north. Nearby rocky inlets and small gorges also provide sheltered conditions favoured by spear-fishing enthusiasts, adding a further, quieter use of the coastline beyond ordinary swimming.

Minimal, Peaceful, and Largely Empty

Agiassos remains almost entirely unorganised: no sunbeds or umbrellas are available to rent, and visitors intending to spend the day should bring their own shade and equipment. A small number of tavernas operate in the immediate area, generally serving fresh fish and simple, well-regarded local dishes alongside genuinely good views over the bay, and a modest range of accommodation, rooms, studios and a small hotel or two, has developed nearby in recent years without ever approaching the density found at Naxos's more central resorts. The overall impression, repeated across nearly every account of the beach, is of a place that stays deserted even in the height of summer, valued specifically by visitors seeking distance from the busier parts of the island.

Getting There

Two routes lead to Agiassos: a scenic, largely paved road via the village of Sagri, following the Agiassos valley down past Bazeos Tower, and a rougher, partly unpaved road approaching from the direction of Pyrgaki. Both are manageable by ordinary car, though a rental car or scooter is strongly advisable, since public bus service to Agiassos is limited, historically running only a handful of times a day and, in some accounts, only during the height of the summer season. Given the beach's genuine remoteness, checking current timetables before relying on the bus is essential.

Nearby: The True End of the Road

Agiassos marks the practical end of Naxos's long southwestern beach corridor, with Pyrgaki, Alyko, Kedros, Glyfada, Kastraki and Mikri Vigla, all covered elsewhere on this blog, tracing the route back up the coast toward Naxos Town. Inland, the village of Sagri and Bazeos Tower sit at the top of the same valley that leads down to the bay, while the ruins of Apaliros, the Byzantine capital Sanudo marched on after landing here, lie a further, less-visited detour into the island's interior for anyone interested in following the full arc of the story this beach began.

 

Most visitors who make it as far as Agiassos are already looking for exactly what they find: an empty beach, a sheltered bay, and very little else besides a good taverna and the sound of the water. Few of them realise they're swimming in the same bay a Venetian fleet once used to launch a conquest that shaped every castle, tower and monastery covered elsewhere in this series. Agiassos keeps that history quietly, the way it keeps almost everything else — without insisting anyone notice.
