KEDROS: THE BEACH THE CEDAR FOREST WAS NAMED FOR

 

Kedros lies at the northernmost point of the Aliko peninsula, on Naxos's southwestern coast, adjoining Hawaii Beach and forming part of the same protected coastal landscape covered in more detail elsewhere on this blog. Despite being, by most accounts, the largest single beach along the entire Aliko coastline, Kedros remains one of the least visited, a direct consequence of its remoteness: reaching it requires a genuine walk through sand, dunes and forest rather than a straightforward drive to a car park, and that one obstacle has been enough to keep it consistently quieter than almost anywhere else in the area.

Named for the Forest Behind It

The beach takes its name directly from kedros, the Greek word for cedar, referring to the dense forest of cedar trees, in botanical terms a species of juniper, that grows across the dunes immediately behind the shoreline. This is the same Kedrodasos, or cedar forest, covered in greater detail in this blog's article on Alyko: a roughly 200-acre expanse of centuries-old junipers rising directly out of the sand, protected under the European Union's Natura 2000 network for its ecological significance. At Kedros specifically, that forest sits closer to the water and more directly frames the beach itself than at almost any other point along the peninsula, giving this particular stretch of sand a more enclosed, sheltered feel than its more open neighbours.

A Beach You Have to Walk To

Unlike much of Naxos's western coastline, Kedros has no direct road access or car park of its own. Visitors typically reach it via the wider Aliko access point, near a food truck that marks the general entrance to the peninsula, before walking north through the dune system and cedar forest for a sustained stretch of sand and uneven ground to reach the beach itself. That walk, while not especially difficult, is long enough to filter out casual, drive-up visitors almost entirely, and it is the single biggest reason Kedros has kept the secluded, uncrowded character that distinguishes it even from its already-quiet neighbours elsewhere on the peninsula.

Naxos's Most Consistently Naturist Beach

That seclusion has made Kedros, by a considerable margin, the beach most strongly associated with naturism anywhere on Naxos. Multiple independent visitor accounts describe the beach as overwhelmingly, and at times almost entirely, clothing-optional, a reputation consistent enough that it appears on mapping services specifically labelled as a naturist beach. Visitors uncomfortable with that atmosphere are generally better served at Hawaii Beach immediately adjoining it, or at the main Aliko beach further south, both of which see a more conventional mix of visitors. For those specifically seeking it out, Kedros is consistently ranked among the most reliable naturist beaches on the island, drawing a relaxed, low-key crowd of couples, families and long-term repeat visitors rather than large groups.

Fine Sand, Dunes and a Wild Shoreline

The beach itself is sandy underfoot both above and below the waterline, backed by rolling dunes and scattered rock, with water running a clear aquamarine close to shore. Its scale, the largest beach on the Aliko coastline by most measurements, gives visitors considerably more room to spread out than the smaller coves nearby, reinforcing the sense of a beach built for solitude rather than density. The overall character is consistently described as wild and undeveloped: fine sand, dunes, exposed rock, and very little else in the way of built infrastructure.

No Shade, No Facilities: Come Prepared

Kedros offers no amenities of its own whatsoever: no sunbeds, no umbrellas, no toilets and, crucially, no natural shade directly on the sand itself, since the cedar forest sits behind the dunes rather than over the beach proper. Visitors are strongly advised to bring their own parasol, water and food, since nothing is available for purchase at the beach, and the walk back to the nearest food truck near the main Aliko access point, let alone the nearest sit-down taverna in the village of Pyrgaki behind the peninsula, is not a short one. This is, deliberately or otherwise, a beach for visitors willing to be self-sufficient for the day in exchange for genuine quiet.

Getting There

Kedros sits roughly eighteen kilometres from Naxos Town, reached via the same coastal route serving Agios Prokopios, Agia Anna, Plaka, Kastraki and Glyfada before arriving at the wider Aliko peninsula. A local bus service runs from the port at Naxos Town to Aliko several times a day in season, though given Kedros's own distance on foot from the main access point, checking the return timetable carefully before setting out is essential; missing the last bus back from this particular stretch of coast is a genuine inconvenience rather than a minor one. A rental car offers considerably more flexibility, though it still leaves the same walk through the dunes to actually reach the beach.

A Note on the Name

Naxos has more than one beach informally known as Kedros, since the word simply means cedar and has attached itself locally to more than one cedar-fringed stretch of coast. A separate, smaller Kedros exists on a peninsula between Agia Anna and Maragas beaches, further north along the coast, also favoured by naturists and located near the chapel of Agios Nikolaos and a distinctive rock formation known locally as the stone shark. The Kedros described in this article is the larger, better-known beach at the northern end of the Aliko peninsula; visitors searching for either should confirm which one a given recommendation, map pin or bus stop actually refers to, since the two are entirely separate beaches roughly ten kilometres apart.

Nearby: The Rest of the Aliko Peninsula

Kedros adjoins Hawaii Beach immediately to its south, itself known for a more dramatic approach down steep cliffs and equally fine sand, while the main Aliko beach, Mikro Alyko and the wider Kedrodasos forest, all covered in this blog's dedicated Alyko article, continue the peninsula further south still. Glyfada and Kastraki lie within easy reach to the north along the same coastal road, rounding out the far southwestern corner of Naxos into one continuous, increasingly wild run of coastline.

 

Most of Naxos's beaches ask a visitor to choose between convenience and seclusion. Kedros removes the choice entirely: there is no easy version of reaching it, no shortcut around the walk through the dunes, and very little waiting at the other end besides sand, cedar and quiet. For visitors who have already worked their way through the island's more accessible coastline and are looking for the point where the road, and the crowd, genuinely runs out, this is very often it.
