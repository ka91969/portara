Tripodes (Vivlos), Naxos: The Village of the Three Windmills

Tripodes, also called Vivlos, is one of the largest villages in southwestern Naxos, about 8 to 9 kilometres from Naxos Town on the road to the Plaka beach area. It is recognised by three windmills on a ridge above the village and is surrounded by the Plaka plain, a productive farming area known for the Naxos potato. Its permanent population is reported at over 800, which is high for an inland village on the island.

TRIPODES (VIVLOS), NAXOS: THE VILLAGE OF THE THREE WINDMILLS

 

Tripodes lies in the south-west of Naxos, on the road that runs from Naxos Town towards Plaka and the beaches of the island’s western coast. It is an agricultural village with a larger and more settled population than most inland settlements, and it has two names in use. Tripodes is the traditional name and carries most of the folklore; Vivlos is the name used on road signs. Both appear in guides and maps, so both are used in searches and directions.

Location and Setting

The village is about 8 to 9 kilometres from Naxos Town, depending on the source, and a short distance inland from Plaka beach. One source gives its altitude as about 130 metres. It stands on a ridge above the Plaka plain, and the three windmills on the hill are its landmark; the simplest way to find the village is to look for them. The population is reported as more than 800 permanent residents, rising to over 1,000 in high summer, and local sources describe the village as one of the more lively on the island, with cafes, traditional tavernas, a bakery and a small number of hotels. Administratively it belongs to the municipality of Naxos and Lesser Cyclades.

The Name: Several Explanations

Tripodes means “three-legged things” or “tripods” in Greek. Local sources agree that the origin of the name is not known with certainty, and several explanations circulate. One holds that the village stands on a stony ridge that spreads in three directions, towards Plaka, Kechires and Sangri; this version is attributed to the Naxian folklorist Michael Kontelieris (1893–1939). Another points to contracts that list three-legged stools as part of women’s dowries. A third says the name was brought by Cretan refugees from a village called Tripodo near Heraklion, during the period of pirate raids. A romantic story involves a princess who watched the stars from a tower while seated on a three-legged stool. These are traditions, not documented etymologies, and are best read as folklore.

The alternative name Vivlos (Greek for “book”) is the one used on official signs. Sources say little about its origin, and no documented explanation is available.

A Settlement That Moved Inland

As in other Naxian villages, local accounts describe an earlier settlement nearer the coast. The ruins of the Plaka Tower, also called Paleopyrgos and in popular tradition “Ariadne’s Tower”, and the chapel of Agios Mattheos, reported to stand on the site of an ancient temple, have led to the suggestion that the first settlement was on the Plaka shore. Raids by pirates are said to have pushed the inhabitants inland, and the present site is said to have been fixed during Frankish rule in the Middle Ages. A further early settlement is placed at Kechires, where the ruins of the tower of Agios Georgios stand above a ravine; it is described as a defensive outpost linked to a tower of the Ga family, whose member Ioannis Ga is named as a notable notary of Naxos. Much of this is based on local tradition and on visible ruins rather than on published archaeological study, so dates are approximate.

Windmills, Churches and Village Sights

The three windmills on the ridge are the village’s trademark and are visible from much of the surrounding plain. Some guides describe them as ruins and others as restored landmarks, so their condition should be confirmed on site. On entering the village, visitors pass the monastery church of Panagia Tripodiotissa, dated by one source to the 16th century. Tripodes is known for its many small churches, with some guides counting around forty in the village and its countryside, many of them Byzantine or post-Byzantine in origin. Other places named in local guides include Stavropigi and the chapel of Agios Andreas. At the village exit towards Ano Sangri, the stone ruins of the old public washhouse (plintirio) are described as a former social centre for the women of the village. The narrow, paved streets and whitewashed houses make up the rest of the village fabric.

The Plaka Plain and Local Produce

Tripodes owes its prosperity to the plain of Plaka, a productive agricultural area that supports the Naxos potato, along with barley, vegetables, fruit and grapes. Vines in the area produce local wine, and potatoes from Naxos are among the island’s best-known agricultural products. Agriculture, livestock and tourism are described as the main sources of income. Taverna menus in the village generally draw on this produce.

Visiting Tripodes

Tripodes is on the main road to the southwest beaches and is reached by car or taxi from Naxos Town in a short drive of roughly 15 to 20 minutes under normal traffic; public buses serve the southwest of the island, but routes and timetables are seasonal and should be checked at the terminal in Naxos Town. The village can be explored on foot in about an hour, with the windmill ridge the most useful vantage point. It combines easily with the Plaka beach area, with Sangri and the Temple of Demeter (see the Sangri guide), and with the Tragea valley villages (Halki, Damarionas, Damalas and Moni). For the historic centre of Chora, see the guide to the Kastro of Naxos. Churches are small and may be locked outside service times; modest dress is expected inside.

Quick Answers

Is Tripodes the same as Vivlos? Yes. Tripodes is the traditional name and Vivlos is the name on road signs.

What is Tripodes known for? It is known for its three windmills, many small churches, and the surrounding Plaka plain, where Naxos potatoes are grown.

How far is Tripodes from Naxos Town? About 8 to 9 kilometres, roughly 15 to 20 minutes by car in normal traffic.

What does the name mean? It means “three legs” or “tripods”; the exact origin is uncertain and several traditional explanations exist.

 

Tripodes is a working village rather than a museum, and its significance lies in that: a large, stable farming community with a landscape of windmills, small churches and fertile fields that links the old centre of Naxos Town with the western beaches.
