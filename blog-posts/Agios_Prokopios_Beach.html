AGIOS PROKOPIOS BEACH: NAXOS'S FINEST STRETCH OF COAST

 

Agios Prokopios sits on the western coast of Naxos, roughly five to six kilometres from the centre of Naxos Town, positioned between the beaches of Agios Georgios to the south and Agia Anna to the north. It is widely regarded as the best beach on the island and one of the most decorated in the country, having been named Greece's third-best beach in various national rankings and placed among the top ten beaches in Europe, with the British newspaper The Telegraph separately listing it among the ten best beaches in the Mediterranean for family holidays. A regular recipient of the Blue Flag award, the international eco-label recognising excellence in water quality, environmental management and safety, Agios Prokopios has built its reputation on a straightforward but rare combination: real scale, genuinely clear water, and a setting sheltered enough to make swimming comfortable on days when much of the rest of the island is battling the wind.

The Chapel Behind the Name

The beach takes its name from a small, whitewashed chapel dedicated to Saint Prokopios, which stands toward the beach's southern end. The chapel's feast day, celebrated locally each year on July 8th, remains a modest but genuine occasion in the surrounding village, a reminder that behind the beach's international rankings sits an otherwise unassuming rural parish. The wider settlement of Agios Prokopios that has grown up behind the dunes is itself a small, formally recognised village, recording a population of 182 residents at the 2021 census, though the numbers swell considerably through the summer season with visitors staying in the area's hotels, villas and apartments.

The area's history predates its current tourism-driven identity by some way. Agios Prokopios is understood to have once functioned as a minor port for vessels arriving from the direction of Turkey, and, according to local tradition, was periodically used by pirates operating in these waters during earlier centuries — a history some accounts connect to an old shipwreck still visible near the rocks toward the end of the beach.

Sand, Water and Setting

The beach extends for approximately 1.5 kilometres of coarse-grained, pale golden sand, giving way at its landward edge to a system of low, gently rolling dunes rather than a hard promenade or built embankment. The seabed shelves gradually rather than dropping away sharply, a characteristic that makes the water particularly well suited to families and to swimmers who prefer a long, easy wade before reaching real depth. Water clarity is consistently cited as the beach's defining feature: close to shore, the sea runs a pale, almost luminous turquoise, deepening progressively further out into a richer sapphire blue, an effect vivid enough that several sources describe the overall impression as closer to a vast natural swimming pool than a conventional stretch of open coastline.

Much of that calm is a function of geography rather than luck. Naxos is regularly affected by the meltemi, the strong seasonal north wind that sweeps the Aegean through the summer months, but Agios Prokopios's westward-facing position, together with the shelter provided by the low hill of Stelida immediately to its north, leaves the bay comparatively protected, with gentle waves even on days when other parts of the island's coastline are considerably rougher. That same hill, Stelida, is itself a site of major archaeological significance, having yielded evidence of hominin activity stretching back roughly 200,000 years, making it one of the oldest documented sites of human presence anywhere in Greece.

The Red Lake and Its Herons

Toward the northern end of the beach lie the remains of an old saltpan, once a genuine part of the island's rural economy, now surviving as a shallow body of water known locally as Kokkini Limni, the "Red Lake," named for the distinctive pink-red tint the water periodically takes on. The lake functions today as a small but genuine wildlife habitat, most notably serving as a resting point for herons and other migratory birds pausing here before continuing their seasonal journey on to Africa — a quietly significant ecological footnote to a beach more commonly discussed in terms of sand quality and water clarity.

Organisation and Amenities

Agios Prokopios operates for the most part as a fully organised beach, with sunbeds and umbrellas arranged along the greater part of its length and lifeguards on duty through the main season. A quieter, less developed stretch remains at the beach's western and southern edge, near the chapel itself, offering a genuine alternative for visitors who prefer to avoid the more built-up central section. The bulk of the area's tourism infrastructure — tavernas, cafés, mini markets, and car and motorbike rental agencies — clusters along the beach's eastern flank and through the village behind the dunes, with a wide range of rooms, studios, apartments and villas available in the surrounding area for visitors choosing to base themselves here for a longer stay. A variety of water sports is also on offer directly from the beach, including windsurfing, kayaking, jet-skiing, sea bicycles and beach volleyball, catering to a noticeably broader range of activity than many of the island's quieter, more low-key beaches.

Getting There

Agios Prokopios is easily reached from Naxos Town by car, taxi, bicycle or on foot for more determined walkers, with the distance most commonly cited at between five and six kilometres depending on the exact starting point in town. Regular public buses run from Naxos Town's central station directly to Agios Prokopios throughout the day in season, making it one of the most straightforward beaches on the island to reach without a rental car. Ample parking is available near the main beach entrances during the summer months, though arriving earlier in the day is generally advisable during the busiest weeks of July and August.

Nearby: Completing the Coastline

Agios Prokopios sits within easy reach of several of the island's other significant western-coast beaches, most notably Agia Anna immediately to the north and Agios Georgios, Naxos Town's own town beach, a short distance to the south, allowing visitors to sample a genuine variety of coastal settings within a single short stretch of coastline. The archaeological site of Stelida, covered elsewhere on this blog, rises directly behind the beach's northern end and makes for a natural, and considerably cooler, addition to a day otherwise spent by the water, while Naxos Town itself, with the Portara, the Kastro and the full range of the island's dining and shopping, remains only a short drive away.

 

Few beaches manage to combine genuine scale, consistently clear water, reliable shelter from the wind and a functioning ecosystem all within the same kilometre and a half of coastline. Agios Prokopios does, and its long list of Blue Flag awards and international rankings reflects a reputation earned on fundamentals rather than marketing — sand, water and setting, in roughly that order of importance, sustained across a beach large enough that it rarely feels as crowded as its popularity might suggest.
