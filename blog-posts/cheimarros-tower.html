Cheimarros Tower: The Cyclades' Best-Preserved Ancient Watchtower

On a lonely hillside between two ravines in southeastern Naxos, a cylindrical marble tower has stood, in one form or another, for roughly 2,300 years — not a Venetian defence against pirates, but something considerably older: a genuine relic of Hellenistic-era warfare, built centuries before Rome ever set foot in the Aegean, and still standing taller than almost any other tower of its kind anywhere in Greece.

CHEIMARROS TOWER: THE CYCLADES' BEST-PRESERVED ANCIENT WATCHTOWER

 

Halfway between the summit of Mount Zas, the tallest peak in the Cyclades, and the sea, on a low hill pinched between two seasonal ravines in the southeastern corner of Naxos, stands one of the island's most quietly extraordinary monuments. Cheimarros Tower — Pyrgos Cheimarrou in Greek — is a cylindrical Hellenistic-period fortification built almost entirely from massive blocks of Naxian marble, still standing to a height of roughly 14 to 15 metres nearly 2,300 years after it was raised. Local tradition and several tourism authorities on the island rank it, alongside the Portara, as one of Naxos's two defining architectural landmarks. Unlike the Portara, however, Cheimarros Tower has nothing to do with a temple, a god, or an unfinished dedication. It was built, in a genuinely turbulent period of Greek history, for war.

A Tower Named for the Streams Around It

The tower takes its name directly from its setting: chimarros is the Greek word for a winter torrent or seasonal stream, and the site sits on a hill flanked by two such ravines, the Cheimarros to the east and the Petronia to the west, roughly fourteen kilometres from the mountain village of Filoti along the road toward the bay of Kalados. The location is no accident. From this elevated position, the tower commands sweeping views over the surrounding countryside toward both the interior of the island and the sea to the south — precisely the kind of vantage point a genuine military watch installation would require.

Built in a Genuinely Dangerous Century

Archaeologists generally date Cheimarros Tower to the late 4th century BC, placing its construction squarely within the Hellenistic period that followed the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC — a chaotic era in which Alexander's former generals, the Diadochi, fought a series of prolonged wars against one another for control of the fragments of his empire, with the Cyclades repeatedly caught between competing naval powers. It was precisely this kind of sustained, large-scale military instability, rather than the smaller-scale piracy that would later drive Naxos's Venetian-era tower-building tradition, that scholars believe prompted the construction of substantial defensive architecture like Cheimarros across the Aegean islands during this period. Interestingly, research into comparable towers elsewhere in the Cyclades has shown that some examples of this general type were already being built as early as the 6th century BC, meaning their purpose likely varied considerably by period and location even where their basic circular design remained similar.

An Engineering Achievement in Marble

What makes Cheimarros genuinely exceptional is not simply its age but its condition. Similar circular towers are known from across the Aegean — researchers have documented as many as seventy-three on the island of Kea alone, with further examples on Kythnos, Serifos, Amorgos, Mykonos, Paros and Tinos — but in the great majority of cases, only the stone foundations survive, the upper structure long since collapsed or quarried away for other building projects. Cheimarros is a rare and remarkable exception: its exterior remains standing to an impressive height, with only its interior masonry showing significant collapse and instability in places.

The tower's base measures roughly 9.2 metres in diameter, and its walls were built using a genuinely sophisticated double-wall technique: two separate faces of dressed marble blocks, tied together at intervals by long bondstones running through the entire thickness of the wall, with the resulting cavity between the two faces packed with mud and rubble for additional strength and insulation. Internally, four separate storeys were connected by a marble staircase built directly into the thickness of the wall itself, rising toward an estimated original height that different sources place at somewhere between 17 and 20 metres — considerably taller than the roughly 14 to 15 metres that survive today. The tower's defensive character is unmistakable in its remaining openings: a single entrance on the south side, one window positioned some 10 metres above ground level, and a series of narrow arrow-slits piercing the walls at intervals, clearly designed for observation and defence rather than daily domestic comfort. The whole structure originally stood within a further, almost square defensive enclosure wall measuring roughly 35 metres along each side, extending the tower's protected footprint well beyond the cylindrical building itself.

Watchtower, Refuge, or Both? An Unresolved Debate

Despite the tower's remarkable state of preservation, archaeologists have never definitively settled what, exactly, it and its many Aegean counterparts were actually for. The long-standing scholarly view holds that towers of this kind functioned either as watchtowers, positioned to spot approaching danger and relay warning across a wider network, or as places of refuge where the local population could shelter during the periodic military upheavals of the Hellenistic era — and quite possibly, over the course of the tower's working life, as both at different times. What most researchers agree on is scale: a fortification of Cheimarros's size and evident engineering sophistication is very unlikely to represent the independent work of local farmers acting alone, and more plausibly formed part of a wider, centrally organised defensive system, coordinated at the level of the ancient city-state of Naxos itself rather than built piecemeal by scattered rural communities.

A Second Life: Roman Workshops Inside the Walls

Cheimarros Tower's working life did not end with the Hellenistic period. Archaeological investigation within the square fortification enclosure surrounding the tower has revealed clear evidence of Late Roman-period reuse of the site, including the remains of workshops — among them olive presses — and storerooms built directly within the old defensive perimeter. Centuries after its original military purpose had presumably lapsed, the site's already-fortified footprint continued to prove useful for an entirely different, more agricultural function, a modest but telling example of the same pattern of long-term reuse visible at so many of Naxos's ancient sites.

Study, Protection and a Place on the World Stage

Cheimarros Tower attracted attention from European travellers to the Aegean well before formal archaeological study began, and the British archaeologist J.P. Droop published one of the earliest dedicated scholarly studies of the monument in 1923. Formal legal protection followed considerably later: the site was officially declared an archaeological site by the Greek state in 2011, and it has since undergone conservation and restoration work aimed at stabilising its fragile interior masonry. Its significance extends well beyond Naxos itself — Cheimarros Tower appears on Greece's tentative UNESCO World Heritage List as part of a proposed serial nomination titled "Ancient Towers of the Aegean Sea," presented alongside comparably impressive examples such as the tower of Agios Petros on the neighbouring island of Andros, itself preserved to a height of around 21 metres and widely regarded, together with Cheimarros, as one of the two best-preserved Hellenistic towers anywhere in the Cyclades.

Visiting Cheimarros Tower Today

The tower sits in a genuinely remote, unspoiled setting roughly fourteen kilometres from Filoti along the road toward Kalados and Panermos bay on the island's southern coast, reachable by car via a mostly paved but increasingly rural route as it approaches the site itself. There is no ticket booth, gate or fixed opening hours; the monument sits in open countryside and is freely accessible at any time, though its remoteness means a car is effectively essential, since it lies well outside the range of Naxos's local bus network.

The reward for the drive is a monument experienced almost entirely without the crowds that gather at Naxos's more central sights — visitors can walk right up to the tower's base, take in the sheer scale of its marble construction close up, and appreciate, from ground level, engineering that has genuinely outlasted almost every other structure of its kind in the entire Aegean. The surrounding landscape, dry hillside scrub framed by the two ravines that give the tower its name, with Mount Zas rising inland and the sea visible toward the south, adds a real sense of isolation that few of Naxos's better-known monuments can match.

Nearby: Completing a Very Different Kind of Tower Tour

Cheimarros makes a natural, illuminating contrast with Bazeos Tower, elsewhere in this series: where Bazeos and Naxos's other Venetian-era fortified monasteries were built roughly two thousand years later, in direct response to Aegean piracy under Venetian and then Ottoman rule, Cheimarros represents the same basic defensive impulse expressed in an entirely different era, technology and political context — Hellenistic warfare between the successor kingdoms of Alexander's empire, rather than corsair raids on isolated rural property. Visiting both towers in the course of a longer stay offers a genuinely rare opportunity to compare Aegean defensive architecture across a gap of roughly two millennia, using two of the best-preserved examples from either end of that timeline. The nearby mountain village of Filoti, Naxos's largest inland settlement and the traditional starting point for hikes up Mount Zas, makes a natural base for a day that takes in both the tower and some of the island's best hiking country, while the beaches of Kalados and Panermos on the southern coast offer an easy way to end the day once the drive back down from the hills is done.

 

Most visitors to Naxos encounter towers only in their Venetian form — fortified monasteries and noble refuges built against a very particular, late-medieval kind of danger. Cheimarros is a reminder that the island's instinct to build tall, thick-walled marble structures on defensible high ground is very much older than that, stretching back through the wars of Alexander's successors and, in some Aegean examples, even further still. Nearly two and a half thousand years on, still standing taller than almost any comparable tower in Greece, it remains one of the most tangible, least-visited pieces of Naxos's ancient military history.
