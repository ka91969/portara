Schinoussa: The Small Cyclades' Softest Island

Low green hills, cultivated hollows, a harbour barely large enough to call itself one, and, anchored just offshore through the summer months, a genuinely disproportionate number of very expensive private yachts. Schinoussa has fewer than 250 year-round residents and almost nothing built for visitors, and has somehow become one of the most quietly sought-after small islands in the entire Aegean anyway.

SCHINOUSSA: THE SMALL CYCLADES' SOFTEST ISLAND

 

Schinoussa lies south of Naxos in the Small Cyclades, between Iraklia and Koufonisia, covering barely 8.5 square kilometres and home to roughly 225 to 250 permanent residents depending on the year. Where Iraklia trades on genuine remoteness and Koufonisia has grown into the group's most popular name, Schinoussa occupies a quieter niche of its own: a noticeably softer, greener landscape than its rockier neighbours, and, almost paradoxically for an island with barely a handful of hotels, one of the more established private yacht anchorages anywhere in the central Aegean.

A Name of Uncertain Origin

Schinoussa's name, rendered in various transliterations including Schoinoussa and Skhinoussa, has no single confirmed derivation. The two leading theories trace it either to a corruption of an ancient name, Echinousa, or to a Venetian nobleman named Schinoza associated with the island during the long centuries of Venetian influence across the Cyclades. Either origin would fit comfortably alongside the naming patterns already documented elsewhere on this blog in relation to Naxos's own Venetian-era history.

Getting There: Plan to Stay the Night

Schinoussa is reached from Naxos Town by ferry, most reliably aboard the Skopelitis, the local Small Cyclades boat that calls daily except Sunday, with additional faster services running during the summer season. Unlike Koufonisia or even Iraklia, Schinoussa's ferry timetables make a genuine same-day round trip from Naxos difficult to arrange with any real margin for error, and most visitors, and most guides to the island, treat an overnight stay as close to a practical necessity rather than an optional upgrade. Accommodation is genuinely limited, concentrated mainly around the port and in Chora, and booking well ahead of a summer visit is essential rather than advisable.

Three Settlements, One Hidden From the Sea

Schinoussa's population lives across three small settlements. Mersini, the port, amounts to little more than a curve of sand, a row of tamarisk trees and a handful of buildings clustered around the jetty where the ferries dock. Chora, also known as Panagia, sits on a ridge some 1.2 kilometres inland and uphill, a single-street village of whitewashed houses built, in a pattern familiar from Halki and Apano Kastro on Naxos itself, deliberately out of sight from the sea to protect its residents from the pirate raids that plagued this stretch of the Aegean for centuries; today it functions as the island's genuine social centre, its small square filling most evenings with residents and the relatively few visitors staying nearby. Messaria, smaller again, holds only around twenty permanent residents and sits along the path connecting Chora to the island's best-known beach.

A Favourite Anchorage, Quietly

What distinguishes Schinoussa most clearly from its Small Cyclades neighbours is a reputation built almost entirely without infrastructure to support it: the island has become, over recent years, a genuinely favoured summer anchorage for private yachts, with megayachts and, on occasion, private helicopters a recognised if unadvertised feature of its high season. None of this has meaningfully changed daily life on the ground, which remains centred on Chora's evening square and Mersini's handful of tavernas rather than on any visible luxury infrastructure; the appeal, for the vessels that anchor offshore each summer, appears to be precisely the absence of exactly that kind of development, a discretion Schinoussa offers by default rather than by design.

A Supporting Role in a Much Older Story

Schinoussa has been inhabited since antiquity, and its ancient significance, while modest compared with its neighbours, was real: the island played a supporting role in the Early Cycladic culture centred on nearby Keros, the extraordinary ritual sanctuary covered in this blog's guide to Koufonisia, lying only a short sail away. Byzantine-era ceramic finds and the ruins of raided churches point to a long, difficult history of piracy, echoed in the remains of two sea caves near the port once used by raiders and still visitable today by small boat, and in the low ruins of a Venetian-era lookout post above Chora, its position chosen for clear sightlines toward Amorgos. The island's current population descends largely from settlers who arrived from Amorgos in the early nineteenth century, repopulating a landscape that piracy had, at various points, left largely abandoned.

The Best Water Supply in the Small Cyclades

A practical, easily overlooked detail sets Schinoussa apart from its drier neighbours: three natural springs give the island what is generally regarded as the best water supply anywhere in the Small Cyclades, a genuine agricultural and settlement advantage reflected in the cultivated hollows and vegetable gardens visible around Messaria, and very likely a real factor in the island's continuous habitation despite its small size and limited natural harbour.

Fifteen Beaches, Mostly Reached on Foot

Schinoussa holds a genuinely striking number of beaches for its size, with around fifteen scattered sandy coves reachable mainly along dirt tracks and footpaths rather than by road. The sand here runs grey rather than the golden tones familiar from Naxos and Koufonisia, a detail some visitors point to as part of why the island stayed off the main tourist circuit for so long despite the quality of its coastline. Psili Ammos, reached via the path from Messaria, is by a clear margin the island's most visited and most highly regarded beach, though its relative popularity by Schinoussa's own modest standards still leaves it considerably quieter than comparable beaches on neighbouring islands.

A Ninety-Minute Circuit Through the Island's History

A walking circuit of roughly ninety minutes links Chora to Messaria and down to the coast, passing both the pirate-era sea caves near the port and the ruined Venetian lookout above Chora along the way, a genuinely satisfying route for visitors wanting to see the bulk of the island's history and geography in a single unhurried walk. With almost no cars in regular use and everything on the island within comfortable walking distance, Schinoussa rewards exactly this kind of slow, on-foot exploration more naturally than almost anywhere else covered in this series.

Planning the Visit

Given the ferry schedules, Schinoussa is best approached as an overnight or multi-night stay rather than a rushed day trip from Naxos, ideally combined with time on Iraklia or Koufonisia, both covered elsewhere on this blog, given how easily the local Small Cyclades ferry network links all three. Visitors should bring cash, since the island has no bank and only a single ATM, and should book accommodation well ahead of any summer visit, given how limited the available rooms genuinely are relative to demand.

Nearby: Completing the Small Cyclades

Schinoussa sits within the same tight cluster as Iraklia and Koufonisia, both covered elsewhere on this blog, and the uninhabited islets of Keros, Ofidoussa and Vrachonisida Ligari lie within sight of its coastline, adding a further, quieter layer of Early Cycladic archaeology to an island whose own history has always run in the shadow of its more famous neighbours.

 

Most islands that draw private yachts have, by now, built the infrastructure to match: marinas, beach clubs, restaurants with a view built for exactly that clientele. Schinoussa has done almost none of it, and stayed exactly the kind of place a Skopelitis ferry, a single grocery store and a village square that fills up after dinner would suggest. That the two things, quiet exclusivity and near-total lack of development, have turned out to coexist so easily here is, in the end, the entire case for visiting.
