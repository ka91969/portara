The Mycenaean Tomb at Chosti: A Lost Ruler's Grave in the Mountains

In 1908, a farmer working a vineyard terrace above Komiaki broke through the ground into a domed stone chamber no one had opened in over three thousand years. What he found was a Mycenaean tholos tomb, one of only three ever discovered anywhere in the Cyclades — and, oddly, built nowhere near the coastal city it should have belonged to.

THE MYCENAEAN TOMB AT CHOSTI: A LOST RULER'S GRAVE IN THE MOUNTAINS

 

A few kilometres south of Komiaki, also known as Koronida, the northernmost and most mountainous village on Naxos, a small domed chamber of unworked stone sits in an area known locally as Chosti, half-buried in a vineyard terrace called Axós. It is a Mycenaean tholos tomb, a type of monumental beehive-shaped grave built for the elite rulers of Bronze Age Greece, and it is one of only three such tombs ever discovered anywhere in the Cyclades. Its presence here, in a remote highland valley with no known connection to Naxos's only major Mycenaean city, remains one of the more genuinely puzzling questions in the island's ancient history.

One of Three, in the Whole of the Cyclades

Tholos tombs, characterised by a corbelled "false dome" roof and a straight entrance passage, were the most prestigious form of burial available in Mycenaean Greece, reserved specifically for local rulers and powerful dynastic families rather than ordinary members of a community. They are common enough on the Greek mainland, at sites like Mycenae and Pylos, but genuinely rare across the islands of the Cyclades: only three have ever been identified there, at Agia Thekla on Tinos, at Angelika on Mykonos, and here, at Chosti near Komiaki. Of the three, the tomb at Angelika is generally considered the largest, richest and best preserved, while the Naxian example is smaller and has yielded far less in the way of surviving grave goods — though its rarity within the Cyclades as a whole makes it no less historically significant.

A Ruler's Tomb, Far From the Only Known City

What makes the tomb at Chosti especially puzzling is its location. The only major Mycenaean settlement documented anywhere on Naxos lies at Grotta, on the coast beneath modern Naxos Town, covered elsewhere in this series; Chosti sits deep in the island's mountainous northeastern interior, a considerable distance away by any Bronze Age standard of travel. Since tholos tombs of this kind were built exclusively for genuine local rulers, and since the distance between Chosti and Grotta is too great for the coastal city to have plausibly administered a monument this far into the highlands, researchers have proposed a striking alternative explanation: that the fertile valley around Komiaki supported its own independent centre of power during the Mycenaean period, a local lordship in its own right, ruling this rich agricultural highland without direct subordination to the coastal centre at Grotta. If correct, that reading recasts Komiaki not as some remote, peripheral corner of Bronze Age Naxos but as a genuine regional seat of authority in its own right, holding a comparable if smaller-scale status to the coastal capital nearly forty kilometres away.

A Domed Chamber Built Without Mortar

The tomb itself is a compact but genuinely accomplished piece of prehistoric engineering. Its circular burial chamber was built from uncarved local slate and boulders, laid in horizontal courses that gradually narrow inward as they rise, each ring of stone projecting slightly further in than the one beneath it until the walls meet at the top in a false dome, a construction technique known as corbelling and achieved here entirely without mortar, the stones supporting one another purely through their own weight and careful placement. The chamber measures 3.3 metres in diameter at its base and stands 2.4 metres high, a straight, stone-lined passage roughly three metres long, known as a dromos, leading into it from outside. An opening on the tomb's south side, created either by ancient looters or by the same collapse that would later expose it to a modern farmer's plough, shows clear signs of disturbance predating any formal excavation.

Discovered by Accident in 1908

The tomb had been, in its original state, completely covered by an earthen mound, entirely invisible from the surface and indistinguishable from the surrounding vineyard terraces of the Komiaki valley. It remained that way until 1908, when a local farmer working the terrace known as Axós broke through the buried dome while tilling his field, exposing the chamber beneath for the first time in over three thousand years. Subsequent archaeological investigation of the tomb found remarkably little: the grave had already been looted, almost certainly in antiquity, and excavators recovered only a handful of fragments of plain, undecorated pottery, offering little material evidence to work with beyond the architecture of the tomb itself.

A Date That Remains Genuinely Uncertain

Because so little survived inside the tomb, dating it with real precision has proven difficult, and different sources offer somewhat different estimates as a result. Some place its construction toward the end of the 15th century BC, based primarily on the style and technique of the masonry itself; others suggest a later date closer to 1300 BC, within the broader span of the Late Helladic III period that saw Mycenaean civilisation at its height, roughly 1400 to 1200 BC, the era of Agamemnon, the Trojan War, and the great palatial centres of the Greek mainland. What can be said with more confidence is that the tomb belongs somewhere within this general Mycenaean horizon, and that its sophisticated construction technique places it firmly within the same elite architectural tradition found at contemporary tholos tombs on the mainland, adopted here on Naxos by a local ruling family with access to considerable labour and resources.

A Tomb for a King Named Naxos?

Local tradition has occasionally speculated that the tomb might belong to Naxos himself, the mythical hero after whom the island is said to take its name, though this remains folklore rather than anything archaeology can confirm; the poor state of the surviving evidence means the tomb's true occupant, and their actual name, is unlikely ever to be established with certainty. What can be said is that whoever was buried here commanded the resources and status necessary to commission one of the most architecturally demanding tomb types available anywhere in the Mycenaean world, a genuine mark of authority even in the absence of a name to attach to it.

Komiaki: A Village Old Enough to Remember It

The village of Komiaki itself, perched on the ridge of Mount Koronos at an altitude of around 650 metres and the northernmost settlement on Naxos, has been inhabited since prehistoric times, its own name thought to derive from an ancient settlement called Komi. Further tombs from the much earlier Early Cycladic period, dating to roughly 3,500 BC, have also been identified in the area surrounding the village, suggesting a long, if intermittent, history of habitation across this high, fertile valley stretching back well before the Mycenaean tholos tomb was ever built. A Frankish-era fort at the centre of the modern village, along with documentary references to Komiaki dating back to 1537, further indicate that this remote mountain community continued without real interruption through the medieval period and into Ottoman rule.

Visiting the Tomb Today

The tomb at Chosti lies a few kilometres south of Komiaki, in open agricultural land rather than within a formally managed archaeological park. Reaching it typically means following a dirt road from the village and continuing on foot for a short final stretch; signage is minimal to non-existent, and visitors are generally advised to arrive with a good map or GPS coordinates rather than expect to find the site by casual wandering. What survives today shows real signs of its age and disturbance, the roof partially collapsed in places, but the circular chamber, the corbelled masonry of its walls and the stone-lined dromos leading into it remain clearly visible, giving a genuine, tangible sense of the tomb's original construction despite the centuries of exposure and looting it has endured. This is a site for the genuinely curious rather than a polished visitor attraction, and it rewards exactly the kind of traveller willing to track it down on its own difficult terms.

Nearby: Komiaki and the Island's Mycenaean Story

A visit to the tomb pairs naturally with the village of Komiaki itself, home to the post-Byzantine Church of Theoskepasti and a cluster of restored traditional buildings, including a watermill, an olive mill and a wine press. The mining village of Koronos, a short distance away and mentioned elsewhere in this series in connection with Naxos's emery industry, lies within the same mountainous northeastern region. For visitors interested in placing the tomb within the island's fuller Mycenaean story, Grotta, the coastal city the tomb's occupant may well have ruled independently of rather than in service to, and the elite cemeteries at Aplomata and Kamini, both covered elsewhere in this series, offer the clearest points of comparison anywhere on Naxos.

 

Most of Naxos's Mycenaean-era sites cluster around the same stretch of coastline beneath Chora, telling a single, reasonably coherent story of one city's rise. The tomb at Chosti tells a different, more unsettled story: of a ruler who never needed that city's permission to build a monument fit for one, tucked into a mountain valley a farmer's plough would not disturb again for three thousand years.
