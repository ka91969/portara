Apollonas Beach: Sand, Marble Pebbles and the End of the Road

At the northern tip of Naxos, where the coast road finally runs out, a small fishing village wraps itself around a beach that changes character within a few hundred metres: soft sand by the whitewashed houses, giving way to a long shore of white marble pebbles once quarried from the very hills behind it.

APOLLONAS BEACH: SAND, MARBLE PEBBLES AND THE END OF THE ROAD

 

Apollonas Beach fronts the small fishing village of the same name at Naxos's northern tip, roughly thirty to thirty-six kilometres from Naxos Town, depending on the exact route taken. Sources differ slightly on the precise distance, but every account agrees on the more important point: the drive itself, winding through the island's mountainous interior, is as much a part of the experience as the beach waiting at the end of it. Apollonas is, by most measures, the most developed settlement anywhere on Naxos's northern coast in terms of tourism, and yet it has somehow kept the unhurried, faintly time-stopped atmosphere of a genuine working fishing village rather than a resort built for visitors alone.

A Fishing Village Built on Marble

Apollonas today is a compact settlement of whitewashed houses gathered around a small harbour, where painted fishing caiques and nets spread along the pier still make their living from the sea rather than from tourism alone. That harbour has a longer history than its present quiet charm suggests: in antiquity, Apollonas served as a working port for the export of Naxian marble, the same brilliant white stone quarried from the hills immediately behind the village and shipped out across the ancient Aegean. It is a history the beach itself still visibly carries, since much of its shoreline is made up of pale marble pebbles rather than sand.

Two Beaches in One Bay

Apollonas's beach is not a single uniform stretch but a genuine mosaic of textures, changing character as it runs along the length of the village and beyond. Closest to the settlement itself lies a small, well-protected sandy cove, extending for roughly 300 metres, calm and shallow enough to make it the clear favourite among families and the one most visitors choose to actually spend their day on. Continuing further along the same bay, the shoreline shifts into a longer stretch covered in smooth, white, egg-shaped marble pebbles, a striking visual combination of gold sand, white stone and blue sea that several sources single out as one of the more genuinely photogenic beach textures on the island.

A Beach the North Wind Can Shut Down Entirely

Apollonas's exposed position at the very tip of the island leaves it considerably more vulnerable to the elements than its more sheltered neighbours further along the north coast. Strong northern winds regularly sweep this stretch of shoreline, and on the roughest days swimming becomes genuinely inadvisable, the sea turning choppy enough to make the whole point of a beach visit moot. Checking the forecast before setting out is worthwhile advice repeated across nearly every source describing the beach; on a calm day, the same exposure that causes trouble instead offers, as more than one visitor has put it, a dramatic, close-up view of just how rough the open Aegean can genuinely be.

A Small Village, Reasonably Well Equipped

Despite its remote position, Apollonas offers more in the way of amenities than most of the beaches covered elsewhere on this side of the island: showers and a bathroom serve the beach directly, a handful of tavernas and cafés line the shorefront serving fresh, simple food at reasonable prices, and a small minimarket and a scattering of souvenir shops round out the village centre. Equipment rental is available directly on the sand for snorkelling, kayaking and stand-up paddleboarding, giving visitors more to do here than at almost any other beach on Naxos's northern or eastern coasts. A small public parking area and a pier used by both fishing boats and tour boats complete the village's modest but genuine infrastructure.

The Giant Outside the Village, and a Second, Smaller One

Apollonas's single best-known attraction lies only a short walk from the beach itself: the colossal, unfinished Kouros of Apollonas, a 10.5-metre marble figure abandoned in its ancient quarry and covered in detail elsewhere in this series, generally believed by archaeologists to represent Dionysus. Less well known is a second, smaller unfinished kouros, roughly seven metres in length, lying further up the hillside above the village and reachable by following signposted paths from the settlement; other similar statues once found in the surrounding quarries have, over the years, been removed or lost, leaving these two as the survivors of what was clearly a far larger ancient sculptural undertaking.

Getting There

The most commonly recommended route to Apollonas runs from Naxos Town through the villages of Galini and Eggares, both discussed in this series' article on Amitis beach, then on past the Tower of Agia before finally descending into the village itself. The road is paved with asphalt for the entire distance and manageable by ordinary car or motorbike, though the drive is long and genuinely winding; public buses also serve the route, often as part of full-day guided tours covering several of the island's major sights along the way.

Nearby: The Far North of Naxos

Apollonas sits at the natural endpoint of the same northern coastal corridor covered elsewhere in this series, with Amitis and Agii Theodori lying back along the route toward Naxos Town, and Agios Fykas a further detour along the coast toward Komiaki. Abram Beach and the ruins of the Tower of Agia both lie within easy reach for visitors basing themselves in the village, rounding out a corner of the island that rewards the long drive with a fishing village, two ancient marble giants, and a beach unlike any other on Naxos.

 

Most visitors who make it as far as Apollonas already know why they're going: the Kouros, mostly, and the promise of a drive that feels like it earns its own destination. What they generally discover alongside it is a beach that quietly does double duty, sand for the children and marble pebbles for everyone else, in a village that has managed, against the general trend of Naxos's tourism, to stay almost exactly what it always was.
