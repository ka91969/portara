Bazeos Tower: From Pirate-Age Monastery to Naxos's Stage for the Arts

In the fertile valley of Ano Sagri, a fortified stone tower has spent four centuries changing jobs: monastery, refuge from pirates, potters' workshop, family summer house, and now, every July and August, one of the most atmospheric concert venues in the Cyclades. Bazeos Tower is Naxos's clearest lesson in how a single building can outlive every purpose it was built for.

BAZEOS TOWER: FROM PIRATE-AGE MONASTERY TO NAXOS'S STAGE FOR THE ARTS

 

Twelve kilometres inland from Naxos Town, on the road that climbs from Chora toward the mountain villages of Halki and Filoti, the landscape opens into the fertile Tragaea plain: olive groves stretching toward low hills, the marble columns of the ancient Temple of Demeter visible in the middle distance, and, standing squarely over the valley near the village of Ano Sagri, a heavy, fortified stone tower that looks, at first glance, more like a small castle than a monastery. That is precisely what Bazeos Tower once was — and it has been several other things besides.

A Building Type Born of Fear

To understand Bazeos Tower, it helps to understand why buildings like it exist across Naxos at all. From 1207, when the Venetian nobleman Marco Sanudo established the Duchy of the Archipelago with Naxos as its capital, the island lived under a feudal system that persisted, in modified form, even after the Ottoman conquest of the Aegean displaced direct Venetian rule in the mid-16th century. Throughout this long period, roughly the 13th to the 18th century, the Aegean was also plagued by near-constant piracy — opportunistic raiders and, at times, notorious corsair fleets — that made isolated rural property a genuine liability. In response, Naxos's landowning families, and its religious institutions, built dozens of fortified tower-houses across the island: thick-walled, defensible structures that could double as a residence, storehouse, place of worship, or all three at once, and that could shelter people and property through a raid rather than simply losing everything to it. Bazeos Tower is one of the largest and best preserved examples of this distinctly Naxian building tradition.

The Monastery of Timios Stavros

Built in its present form around 1600, and bearing a carved inscription dated 1679 above the door of one of its cells, Bazeos Tower began its documented life as the fortified monastery of Timios Stavros — the "Holy Cross." It was, in the local terminology, a tower-monastery: a hybrid institution that functioned simultaneously as a working religious house and as a defensive stronghold for the surrounding rural community. The nearby village of Sagri is itself thought to preserve a corrupted echo of "Sainte Croix," the French rendering of the same dedication, a small linguistic fossil of the monastery's long presence in the valley.

The tower's layout still reflects this dual monastic-defensive purpose. It is a two-storey building enclosed by a heavy stone perimeter wall that defines the entire complex: a main open courtyard providing the sole route to the tower's main entrance, a storage area and small chapel at ground level, a central hall surrounded by a refectory, kitchens and monks' cells on the first floor, and, on the second floor, a large reception room bordered by a further kitchen and an open-air courtyard with sweeping views out across the valley. Monks lived, prayed, ate and, if necessary, defended the site within this single, self-contained stone perimeter — exactly the kind of resilient, multi-purpose architecture that a genuinely uncertain age demanded.

Abandonment, the Greek State, and the Potters

Timios Stavros functioned as an active monastery for roughly two centuries, but by the first decades of the 19th century, the last monks had left and the building stood empty. In 1834, in the years following Greek independence, the tower passed into the ownership of the newly formed Greek state along with a great deal of other formerly ecclesiastical property across the country. For several decades after that, rather than standing empty, the tower found an unexpectedly practical second life: it housed local families of potters, who lived and worked within its thick stone walls, using the sheltered courtyard and storage spaces of a former monastery for an entirely secular rural trade.

The Bazeos Family and a Private Restoration

Toward the end of the 19th century, the Greek state sold the tower to the Bassegio family — a name that, over generations on the island, was gradually hellenised to Bazeos, the family name the building carries today. In keeping with the older Venetian-era tradition common to many landowning Naxian families, the Bazeos family kept their primary residence in Chora and used the tower itself as a summer retreat and agricultural storage space, a lower-key existence than its original monastic role but one that, crucially, kept the building inhabited, maintained, and out of ruin.

It was the last descendant of the family who transformed the tower's fortunes decisively, recognising the building's historical and architectural value and funding its restoration privately rather than allowing it to continue simply as a storage annex. The first phase of that restoration was completed in 2000, carried out under the technical supervision of architects Themistocles Bilis and Maria Magnisali of the National Technical University of Athens — a careful, professionally overseen adaptive reuse project that preserved the tower's historic fabric while preparing it for an entirely new function.

A Stage in the Olive Groves: The Naxos Festival

That new function arrived almost immediately. Since 2001, every summer, Bazeos Tower has hosted the Naxos Festival, now one of the island's most established cultural institutions and, by most accounts, its single most serious annual arts event. Through July and August, the tower's stone courtyards fill with classical concerts, theatre performances, literary readings and art exhibitions, staged among centuries-old walls rather than in a purpose-built auditorium — chamber music by candlelight in a monastery courtyard, rather than on a beachfront stage, is very much the point. The building is managed today by the non-profit cultural organisation AION, which programmes the tower's exhibitions and events in collaboration with a range of cultural partners and institutions, including, in various seasons, Athens's Benaki Museum.

The effect of this second life is not incidental to the building's history but a genuine continuation of it: a structure raised to hold a community together through uncertainty now gathers people together for music and art, in the same rooms, behind the same walls, under a different kind of shelter.

Visiting Bazeos Tower Today

Bazeos Tower sits roughly 12 kilometres from Naxos Town, close to the village of Ano Sagri, on the main inland road connecting Chora to Halki and Filoti — an easy, well-signposted stop for anyone exploring the island's interior by car, with dedicated visitor parking just outside the complex. Naxos's public KTEL bus network also serves the area via lines 2, 6 and 7 from the central bus station at the port; because buses do not always stop at every point along the route automatically, it is worth telling the driver in advance that Bazeos Tower is the intended stop.

Inside, visitors can walk through the ground-floor courtyard and chapel, climb to the first-floor hall, refectory and former monks' cells — keep an eye out for the 1679 inscription above one of the cell doors — and finish on the second-floor terrace, whose open-air courtyard offers some of the best panoramic views of the Tragaea valley available from any single building on the island. Exhibition areas typically operate on a seasonal schedule tied to the Naxos Festival's programme, so checking the festival's current listings before visiting is worthwhile, both to see what is showing and to catch a concert or performance if the timing allows — an evening at Bazeos Tower during festival season is a genuinely different experience from a daytime architectural visit, and worth planning around if the dates align with a trip.

Nearby: Completing the Valley

Bazeos Tower sits within one of the richest small clusters of sights on Naxos, and rewards a slower half-day rather than a quick stop. The 6th-century BC Temple of Demeter, an all-marble sanctuary meticulously reconstructed from its own original stones, lies only a short distance away in the same fertile valley, making it easy to pair an ancient harvest-goddess sanctuary with a post-Byzantine fortified monastery in a single outing — two very different religious buildings, separated by over two thousand years, standing almost within sight of one another. The nearby village of Sagri itself holds the former Monastery of Agios Eleftherios, now a folklore museum, along with the remains of other Venetian-era towers and a scattering of Byzantine churches across the surrounding plateau, while the mountain village of Halki, a short drive further inland, adds the Vallindras distillery and the historic church of Panagia Protothronos to a longer day trip through Naxos's rural heartland.

 

Very few buildings on Naxos capture the island's layered history as neatly as Bazeos Tower does in a single visit: a defensive response to real danger, a monastery, a state property, a potters' workshop, a private family retreat, and now a stage for chamber music and contemporary art, all within the same four walls. Where the Portara speaks to ambition cut short and Grotta to a city continuously inhabited since prehistory, Bazeos Tower tells a quieter, more human story — of a building that simply kept being useful, generation after generation, to whoever needed it next.
