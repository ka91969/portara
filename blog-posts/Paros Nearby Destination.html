PAROS FROM NAXOS: THE NEARBY ISLAND WORTH CROSSING FOR

 

Of every island reachable from Naxos, Paros is both the closest and, for a certain kind of traveller, the most immediately rewarding. The two islands sit only eleven nautical miles apart, close enough that a high-speed catamaran covers the distance in as little as twenty to thirty minutes, and close enough that Paros functions less as a separate destination than as a natural extension of a Naxos stay, whether for a single, well-planned day or a considered two- or three-night addition to a longer Cycladic itinerary. What awaits on arrival is an island that has spent the last decade quietly establishing itself as one of the most sophisticated addresses in the Cyclades, without ever fully surrendering the marble-built, Byzantine-layered character that made it worth visiting in the first place.

Getting There: A Short Crossing, Several Ways to Make It

The standard route between the two islands runs by ferry, with the port of Naxos Town connecting directly to Parikia, Paros's capital and main harbour. High-speed catamarans, operated by several companies including Seajets, cover the crossing in as little as twenty to thirty minutes, while conventional ferries take closer to forty-five minutes to just over an hour, with upward of ten daily sailings in each direction during the high season and a reduced but still workable schedule through the rest of the year. Fares are modest regardless of season, and advance booking, while sensible in August, is rarely strictly necessary given how frequently the route runs.

For a more considered arrival, private charter is readily available from Naxos Town's harbour, whether a speedboat transfer timed to a specific dinner reservation in Naousa or a fuller half- or full-day boat charter that treats the crossing itself, and a slow approach along Paros's northern coast, as part of the experience rather than simply a means of getting there. Given the short distance involved, a private crossing to Paros is one of the more easily arranged, and more quietly memorable, ways to begin a day away from Naxos.

Parikia: Marble Lanes and a Church Nearly Seventeen Centuries Old

Ferries from Naxos dock directly in Parikia, Paros's capital, a town built quite literally from its own famous stone: the lanes of the old quarter are paved throughout in local marble, winding between whitewashed houses, a scattering of boutiques and cafés, and the ruins of a Venetian-era castle assembled, in a detail worth pausing over, from blocks of ancient marble recycled from an even older temple to Apollo. At the centre of it all stands Panagia Ekatontapiliani, the Church of a Hundred Doors, one of the most significant surviving Byzantine monuments anywhere in Greece, dating in its earliest form to the fourth century AD and traditionally associated with Saint Helen, mother of the Roman emperor Constantine. Its interior houses a small Byzantine museum, and its courtyard offers a genuinely peaceful counterpoint to the harbour just beyond it. The town's Archaeological Museum, close by, holds finds spanning the Neolithic through Roman periods, including the Nike of Paros and Cycladic figurines from the island's own prehistoric tradition, the same broad current of Early Cycladic culture already well documented on Naxos itself.

Naousa: From Fishing Village to the Cyclades' Most Fashionable Harbour

If Parikia represents Paros's historical centre, Naousa, roughly ten kilometres north, represents where the island's more recent reputation has actually been made. A genuine fishing harbour still operates here, its old shipyard, where wooden caiques were once built by hand, now standing as a quiet reminder of the maritime tradition the town grew from, but the surrounding lanes have filled in recent years with boutique hotels, design-forward wine bars and some of the most talked-about restaurant tables in the Cyclades. The remains of a 15th-century Venetian fortress stand half in the water at the harbour mouth, floodlit and photographed nightly, while the town itself shifts, as more than one visitor has put it, from a relaxed fishing village by day into something considerably more polished after dark. For travellers prioritising where to eat and where to be seen doing it, Naousa is Paros's clear centre of gravity, and increasingly the preferred base for visitors approaching the island at a premium pace.

A Living Marble Tradition, Familiar from Naxos

Paros shares with Naxos a deep and genuinely ancient marble-working heritage, and for visitors already familiar with the quarries at Flerio and Apollonas, covered elsewhere on this blog, the comparison is a natural one to make in person. Parian marble, quarried since antiquity from the Marathi quarry inland from Naousa, was prized above almost any other stone in the ancient Mediterranean for its fine grain and faint translucency, and is understood to be the material from which the Venus de Milo, among other celebrated ancient sculptures, was carved. The quarry itself, roughly one hundred metres long, remains visible today, a short stop on the inland route between Naousa and the mountain village of Lefkes, and offers a genuinely direct point of comparison with the quarrying landscape already explored on Naxos, two islands drawing on the same geological seam of Cycladic marble to very different, and in Paros's case even more widely celebrated, effect.

Lefkes and the Mountain Villages

Inland and uphill from both Parikia and Naousa, the village of Lefkes offers a cooler, quieter counterpoint to the coast, set in an amphitheatrical valley several degrees more comfortable than the shoreline during the height of summer. Its whitewashed lanes, tavernas and traditional kafenia have made it one of the more popular inland stops on the island without ever losing the unhurried character that distinguishes it from the harbour towns. An old marble-paved path, part of what is known locally as the Byzantine Trail, connects Lefkes to the neighbouring village of Prodromos, offering a genuinely pleasant walk through Paros's rural interior for visitors willing to trade a beach afternoon for a slower, more contemplative one. Smaller villages nearby, including Marpissa and the ceramic-making hamlet of Kostos, round out an inland side of the island most day-trippers never see.

The Beaches: From Rock Sculptures to Windsurfing

Paros's coastline offers a genuinely wide range of beach character within a short drive of either main town. Kolymbithres, just outside Naousa, is defined by a striking sequence of wind- and sea-sculpted granite boulders dividing the shoreline into a series of small, sheltered coves, among the most photographed natural formations anywhere in the Cyclades. Golden Beach, on the island's eastern coast, has built a genuine international reputation as a windsurfing destination, hosting championship-level events thanks to consistently strong summer winds. Santa Maria, also on the northeast coast, offers a longer, more spacious stretch of sand well suited to diving, snorkelling and other watersports without feeling as crowded as its popularity might suggest. Visitors approaching Paros for its beaches alone would do well to treat the island's own beach culture as a genuine counterpart to Naxos's, rather than a lesser version of it.

Antiparos: The Smaller Island Next Door

For travellers with a full day or more to spend, the small island of Antiparos lies just off Paros's western coast, reached by a short passenger ferry from the port of Pounda, itself a short taxi ride from Parikia or Naousa. Antiparos has built a considerable reputation of its own in recent years as a discreet, low-key retreat favoured by a number of well-known international visitors, its single main town retaining a quieter, less developed character than either of Paros's principal harbour towns. Combining a stop here with a Paros day already makes for a fuller, more considered excursion than most visitors manage, though it is worth planning the additional crossing with a clear return schedule in mind given the extra leg involved.

Planning the Visit: Day Trip or Short Stay

Paros suits both a well-organised single day and a more relaxed two- to three-night addition to a Naxos-based holiday equally well. A day trip built around an early morning crossing, arriving in Parikia by mid-morning, allows time to see the Panagia Ekatontapiliani and old town, continue on to Naousa for lunch and a look at Kolymbithres, and still make a comfortable evening ferry back to Naxos, particularly on the fastest high-speed sailings. Visitors preferring a slower pace, or specifically drawn to Naousa's evening restaurant and bar scene, are generally better served booking at least one night on Paros itself, with accommodation in Naousa commanding a genuine premium for its harbourfront views and proximity to the town's best tables, while Parikia and the inland villages offer a somewhat gentler price point for an equally memorable stay.

Nearby: The Rest of the Central Cyclades

Paros sits at the centre of a cluster of islands easily combined with a longer Cycladic itinerary based on Naxos, with Antiparos immediately to its west and the Small Cyclades, Koufonisia, Schinousa and Iraklia, within a further short sail to the southeast. For visitors building out a fuller island-hopping itinerary around a Naxos base, Paros remains the natural first stop, both for its proximity and for how completely it rewards even a single well-planned day.

 

Naxos and Paros have spent the last decade drifting toward opposite reputations, one prized for its scale, its marble-built ancient monuments and its comparative quiet, the other for a harbour town that has become genuinely difficult to get a dinner reservation at in August. Visited from a Naxos base, that contrast becomes an asset rather than a choice to make: an easy half-hour crossing separates two of the Cyclades' most complete versions of themselves, and very few Aegean itineraries make better use of a single afternoon than the one spent moving between them.
