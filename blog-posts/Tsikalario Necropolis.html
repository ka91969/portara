At the foot of Apano Kastro, in a quiet valley between Potamia and Tragaea, eighteen circles of standing stones mark a burial custom found nowhere else in the country that invented so many others. A single carved menhir stands at the entrance — the only one of its kind in the entire Balkans — guarding graves nearly three thousand years old.

TSIKALARIO: THE ONLY TUMULUS CEMETERY IN GREECE

 

In the area known as Alonakia, at the southeastern foot of the hill crowned by Apano Kastro, a scatter of stone circles marks one of the most archaeologically distinctive sites anywhere on Naxos. The Necropolis of Tsikalario, named for the small nearby village, is a Geometric-period cemetery unlike any other found in Greece: a burial ground built entirely of tumuli, low mounds each ringed by upright stones, at a moment in Greek history when almost no other community was burying its dead this way. A single standing stone, a menhir, marks its entrance — and it, too, is the only one of its kind anywhere in the Balkans.

Beside Naxos's Strongest Medieval Castle

Tsikalario sits in the valley between Potamia and Tragaea, directly below Apano Kastro, the Venetian-era hilltop fortress covered elsewhere in this series. That proximity is coincidental rather than causal — the necropolis predates the medieval castle above it by close to two thousand years — but it means a visit to one site places the other within easy reach, two entirely different chapters of Naxian history separated by a short walk and roughly twenty-five centuries.

A Burial Custom Found Nowhere Else in Greece

The cemetery dates to the Geometric period, primarily the ninth and eighth centuries BC, with more sporadic use continuing afterward into the Late Archaic and Early Classical periods. What makes it exceptional is not its age but its form: Tsikalario is, on current evidence, the only Geometric-period cemetery anywhere in Greece built with tumulus graves, earthen or stone-bordered burial mounds of a type more commonly associated with other regions of the ancient world entirely. Archaeologists studying the site have pointed to the presence of these quasi-tumulus structures as evidence of possible cultural influence from burial customs further north in Greece, adapted here with genuine local variation rather than copied wholesale — a rare, tangible sign of Early Iron Age Naxos absorbing and reworking ideas from beyond the island rather than developing in isolation.

Eighteen Mounds, Two of Them Empty

Excavation identified eighteen burial mounds at Tsikalario, varying in size from roughly seven to twelve metres in diameter and mostly circular in plan, though two are rectangular. Only the borders of these tumuli survive today, marked by irregular, upright stones set in rings; the mounds of earth or rubble they originally enclosed have long since eroded or been removed. Within many of these outer rings, smaller stone enclosures once surrounded individual graves, a nested structure of burial space within burial space. Two of the eighteen tumuli, curiously, contained no graves at all, holding nothing but stone — almost certainly cenotaphs or memorial markers rather than actual burial places, raising the possibility that some of the dead honoured here were never physically interred at the site at all.

Ash, Fire and Burned Fruit

At the centre of several tumuli, and occasionally just outside the surrounding stone ring, excavators found layers of ash, the residue of fire used in the burial ritual itself. Among the more evocative finds recovered from the graves was burned fruit, left as an offering to the dead and preserved, unintentionally, by the same fire that consumed it — a rare, specific trace of the actual funerary rite carried out here nearly three thousand years ago, in a period of Greek history that otherwise survives almost entirely through pottery and metalwork rather than direct evidence of ritual practice.

Pottery, Gold and Lances

Grave goods recovered from the tombs included pottery, gold jewellery and lances, though the overall haul was smaller than the size of the cemetery might suggest: most of the graves had already been looted long before archaeologists arrived, and even the handful found undisturbed yielded comparatively modest finds. Modern analysis of the pottery assemblage, drawing on roughly two hundred identifiable vases recovered from the site, found a striking near-even split between handmade and wheelmade wares — a detail of real interpretive significance, since handmade pottery in funerary contexts of this period is often associated with cult practice and ancestor veneration, suggesting that at least some of those buried at Tsikalario held meaningfully elevated status within their community. Broader study of the assemblage has also pointed to selective but genuine connections between Naxos and wider Eastern Mediterranean trade networks during the Early Iron Age, positioning this small inland cemetery within a considerably larger web of contact and exchange than its size or remote setting would suggest.

Two Buildings at the Edge of the Dead

At the eastern edge of the site, excavators uncovered the foundations of two rectangular buildings, each containing two or three rooms. Hearths built from vertical stone slabs stood within them, alongside coarse pottery fragments and simple stone tools, though none of this material has allowed archaeologists to determine the buildings' precise function with any confidence. Their position at the margin of the cemetery, and the presence of working hearths, suggests some functional connection to the burial rites carried out nearby — perhaps spaces for preparing funerary offerings or hosting the kind of ritual feasting attested at other Geometric-period cemeteries in the Aegean — though this remains, honestly, more informed speculation than settled fact.

The Menhir at the End of an Ancient Road

From the east, from the direction of Tragaea, an ancient road leads up toward the cemetery, arriving at its entrance beside a single standing stone, or menhir, that marks the site. This menhir is, by current archaeological understanding, the only monument of its kind anywhere in the Balkans, a genuinely singular piece of prehistoric standing-stone tradition in a part of Europe where such monuments are, unlike in Western Europe, extremely rare. Exactly what the stone signified, whether a marker for travellers, a boundary for the sacred ground beyond it, or something more specifically ritual in purpose, is not recorded, but its survival alone makes it one of the more quietly remarkable individual objects anywhere on Naxos.

Excavated by Zafeiropoulou and Doumas

The site was systematically excavated between 1963 and 1966 by the archaeologists Photini Zafeiropoulou and Christos Doumas, the latter a figure who recurs throughout Naxos's Early Cycladic and Early Iron Age archaeology: Doumas would go on to identify the fortified Bronze Age settlement of Korfari ton Amygdalon at Panormos and publish the rock carvings at Korfi Aroniou, both covered elsewhere in this series, before later becoming internationally known for directing the excavation of the Bronze Age town at Akrotiri on Santorini. Zafeiropoulou's own subsequent publications on the site, including a dedicated 1983 study in the journal Magna Grecia and a fuller account of the tumulus necropolis in 2008 to 2009, remain the foundational scholarly references for Tsikalario today, alongside more recent reassessments of the site's pottery and mortuary practice published in international archaeological journals.

A Later Christian Chapter

As at several other ancient sites across Naxos, Tsikalario's story did not end with the close of the Geometric period. An Early Byzantine church was later built in the vicinity, one more instance of a pattern repeated across the island, where later Christian communities built directly among, or on top of, the sacred and funerary sites of a much older Naxos, whether at Yria, at Gyroulas, or here, in a valley already marked out as significant more than a thousand years before the church's own foundations were laid.

Visiting the Necropolis Today

Tsikalario is reached via a footpath from the village of the same name, in central Naxos between the Potamia valley and the Tragaea plateau. An alternative approach runs from the direction of the Elaiolithos marble workshop area, roughly four kilometres away by car, continuing on foot for the final stretch; more adventurous visitors can also reach the area on foot via the marble quarries around Kinidaros, combining the walk with a look at the island's continuing stone-working tradition. The site itself is open, unfenced ground rather than a ticketed archaeological park, and the same walk that brings visitors to the necropolis continues on to the ruins of Apano Kastro on the hilltop above, at an altitude of 420 metres with sweeping views over both the Tragaea and Potamia valleys — making the two sites a natural single excursion rather than two separate trips.

Nearby: Where the Finds Now Live

The portable finds recovered from Tsikalario, its pottery, gold jewellery and other grave goods, are held today in the Archaeological Museum of Naxos, in the Kastro of Naxos Town, alongside the Early Cycladic, Mycenaean and later material covered elsewhere in this series. Visitors interested in the fuller arc of Naxos's Early Iron Age history can pair a visit to Tsikalario with Grotta, whose own Geometric-period tumulus and menhir-marked graves offer a direct point of comparison from the island's coastal centre, a useful reminder that the tumulus-building tradition identified at Tsikalario was not entirely isolated even within Naxos itself.

 

Naxos's archaeology tends to announce itself either through scale, a colossal doorway, an abandoned giant, or through sheer continuity, a city inhabited for millennia. Tsikalario does neither. Its eighteen modest stone circles and single standing stone hold their significance quietly, in the simple fact of being unlike anything else the rest of the country was doing at the same moment — a distinctly Naxian answer to the universal problem of how to bury, mark, and remember the dead.
