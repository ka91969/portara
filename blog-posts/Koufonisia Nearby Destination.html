Koufonisia: The Small Cyclades' Clearest Water

Two small islands, one inhabited and one deliberately left as it was found, sit a short crossing southeast of Naxos, wrapped in some of the most consistently photographed turquoise water in the entire Aegean. A third, uninhabited islet just offshore turns out to hold one of the oldest religious sanctuaries anywhere in the ancient world — older than Delos, and considerably less visited.

KOUFONISIA: THE SMALL CYCLADES' CLEAREST WATER

 

Koufonisia sits in the Small Cyclades, the loose scatter of minor islands between Naxos and Amorgos, and consists in practice of two very different small islands sharing a single name: Pano Koufonisi, also called Ano Koufonisi, home to the group's only village and its roughly three hundred to four hundred permanent residents, and Kato Koufonisi, a short water-taxi ride away and entirely uninhabited except for goats, campers and a single taverna. A third, uninhabited islet a few miles further south, Keros, completes the group with something genuinely unexpected: one of the oldest religious sanctuaries anywhere in the ancient world, older by centuries than Delos itself.

Hollow Islands, Named for Their Caves

The name Koufonisia translates roughly as "hollow islands," a reference, in the most widely repeated explanation, to the numerous sea caves carved into the islands' coastline by centuries of wave action; an alternative theory traces the name instead to koufos limen, an old term for a sheltered harbour, which would describe the islands' well-protected anchorages just as plausibly. Either way, the islands appear under a scatter of variant names, Kofinousa, Cofinissia, Phocussae, in historical sources going back centuries, evidence of just how long this small corner of the Aegean has been noted, if not always heavily visited.

Getting There From Naxos

Koufonisia is reached from Naxos Town by ferry, with faster services covering the crossing in around forty to fifty minutes and the venerable local Skopelitis, the small, much-loved ferry that has connected the Small Cyclades to the wider network for decades, taking closer to an hour and three-quarters on its more leisurely, island-hopping route. All boats arrive at the small port beside Chora, the group's only proper settlement, on the south coast of Pano Koufonisi.

Pano Koufonisi: A Village Without Cars

Pano Koufonisi covers a little under six square kilometres of largely flat terrain, rising to no more than about 114 metres at its highest point, and remains, by design as much as by size, essentially car-free: visitors walk, cycle, or take one of the small local boats, known as lantzas, that shuttle regularly between the port and the island's beaches. The island only received electricity in the 1980s, a detail locals mention less as a hardship endured than as a marker of just how recently Koufonisia's current, gently increasing popularity actually began. Chora itself remains a genuinely small, low-rise village, its harbour filled with the small colourful fishing boats that still supply several of the tavernas lining the waterfront, and a coastal path connects the entire southern and eastern shore in a walk of roughly an hour, taking in most of the island's best beaches along the way.

Pori and the Rest of Pano Koufonisi's Coastline

Pano Koufonisi's beaches, Finikas, Fanos, Italida, the small natural rock pool known as Pisina, and, at the eastern tip of the island, Pori, are collectively regarded as among the finest in the Cyclades. Pori in particular has built a considerable reputation of its own: a long, shallow, brilliantly turquoise lagoon where the water stays waist-deep for tens of metres from shore, backed by pale sand and, on its quieter fringes, an informal naturist section. Being north-facing, Pori can turn genuinely windy when the meltemi picks up, and visitors are generally advised to save it for a calmer day rather than treating it as an automatic first stop. A cluster of sea caves in the cliffs just behind Pori is reachable only by boat, and local operators run short trips specifically to explore them for visitors with an afternoon to spare.

Kato Koufonisi: Left More or Less Alone

Across a channel barely two hundred metres wide, Kato Koufonisi presents a deliberately different experience: no permanent population, no real facilities beyond Venetsanos, a single taverna near the boat landing serving grilled fish beneath the tamarisk trees, and beaches considered by many repeat visitors to surpass even Pano Koufonisi's own. Nero Beach, at the island's far end, is the longest and most striking, a wide sweep of sand backed by cliffs rising some sixty metres and fronted by water clear enough to see the seabed from a passing boat. Detis offers a smaller, quieter alternative, Pezoulia is frequently singled out as the single best swimming spot in the whole group, and Panagia, further along, hosts its own small seasonal taverna through the summer months. Free camping is a long-standing, informally tolerated tradition on Kato Koufonisi, though visitors intending to stay overnight should bring their own water, since the island has no natural springs of its own, along with cash, since no facilities on the islet accept cards.

Keros: An Older, Stranger Sanctuary Than Delos

A short distance south of Koufonisia lies Keros, a rugged, uninhabited island rising to 432 metres at its highest point and closed to casual landing as a protected archaeological site. Excavations beginning in the 1960s uncovered hundreds of Early Cycladic marble and clay figurines here, and years of careful study revealed something genuinely strange about them: the fragments, deliberately broken rather than damaged by looting or natural causes, do not fit back together, even when pieces of the same original figurine can be identified. The current scholarly interpretation holds that communities across the neighbouring islands deliberately broke these figurines as part of a ritual practice, then carried fragments to Keros in a kind of symbolic pilgrimage, likely tied to ideas of cyclical rebirth, over a span of roughly four to five centuries ending around 2000 BC. Keros's connection to the neighbouring islet of Dhaskalio, once joined to it by a now partly submerged strip of land, adds a further, remarkable data point: excavation there uncovered a sixteen-by-four-metre building, the largest known structure from this period anywhere in the Cyclades, likely built to house pilgrims making the same journey. Archaeologists studying the site have described Keros as the earliest known island sanctuary anywhere in the world, comparable in later significance to Delos, covered elsewhere on this blog, but predating it by several centuries. The site connects directly to the wider "Kastri Group" of fortified Early Cycladic settlements discussed in this blog's coverage of Korfari ton Amygdalon near Panormos on Naxos itself, part of the same broad, still-being-understood picture of organised maritime culture and long-distance ritual travel across the Early Bronze Age Aegean.

Pirates, Venetians, and a Late Arrival to the Modern State

Like much of the Cyclades, Koufonisia passed through Venetian and later Ottoman control, and its position and modest size made it, for extended periods, a genuine refuge for pirates and for islanders displaced from elsewhere in the Aegean, including settlers from the Mani peninsula on the Greek mainland. The islands were not formally incorporated into the modern Greek state until 1830, a comparatively late date that reflects just how peripheral this small corner of the Cyclades remained to the major political events shaping the rest of the island group.

Popular, But Not a Party Island

Koufonisia has grown steadily more popular over the past two decades, and late July into mid-August now brings genuine crowds by the standards of a group this size. What has not changed is the island's fundamental character: this is emphatically not a nightlife destination in the manner of Mykonos, covered elsewhere on this blog, but a low-key, fishing-and-swimming culture built around good, simple seafood, quiet tavernas, and a genuinely relaxed pace even at the height of summer.

Planning the Visit

Koufonisia rewards more than a single rushed day, though a well-organised one, taking in Chora, a swim at Pori, and a short water-taxi crossing to one of Kato Koufonisi's beaches, is entirely achievable for visitors based on Naxos with only a day to spare. Those able to stay longer will find accommodation on Pano Koufonisi ranges from simple rooms to increasingly polished small hotels, and a night or two allows time to properly explore both islands' beaches without the pressure of a return ferry schedule. Given Koufonisia's growing popularity, booking accommodation well ahead for July and August sailings is strongly advisable.

Nearby: The Rest of the Small Cyclades

Koufonisia sits within easy reach of Iraklia and Schinousa, the other principal islands of the Small Cyclades group, both reachable by the same local ferry network and each offering a quieter, even less developed counterpart to Koufonisia's own relaxed pace. For visitors who have already explored this blog's coverage of Naxos's own Early Cycladic archaeology, Keros adds a genuinely important further chapter to that same story, situated only a short sail from a Naxos base.

 

Most small Aegean islands ask a visitor to choose between good beaches and genuine historical weight. Koufonisia, almost uniquely, offers both within the same short boat ride: water clear enough to read a coin on the seabed at Pori, an uninhabited islet next door with nothing on it but goats and a single taverna, and, a little further out, a sanctuary old enough to have been ancient history by the time Apollo was ever said to have been born on Delos.
