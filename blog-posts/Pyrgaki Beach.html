PYRGAKI: WHERE NAXOS'S SOUTHWESTERN COAST RUNS OUT

 

Pyrgaki lies at the far southwestern tip of Naxos, roughly nineteen to twenty-one kilometres from Naxos Town depending on the route taken, directly beyond Alyko and effectively marking the point where the island's long run of southwestern beaches comes to its natural end. It is, by most descriptions, an enormous stretch of sand with a genuinely strong sense of open space, set within a small, sheltered cove and looking out across open water toward the islands of Iraklia and Ios in the distance. Despite its considerable size, Pyrgaki is consistently described as one of the quietest, least crowded beaches anywhere on Naxos, a beach people arrive at deliberately rather than pass through on the way to somewhere else.

A Name That Echoes Kastraki's

The name Pyrgaki follows the same naming pattern as Kastraki, elsewhere along this same coastline: it is a diminutive of the Greek word pyrgos, meaning tower, making Pyrgaki, literally, "little tower." Where Kastraki's name ties directly to a specific surviving Venetian structure, the Oskelos Tower a short distance inland, no single named tower at Pyrgaki itself is as clearly documented, though the naming convention itself points to the same broader pattern found across Naxos, where small rural settlements and the beaches beneath them frequently took their names from the watch-towers, fortified mansions, and defensive structures that Venetian-era landowners built across the island's countryside. The settlement of Pyrgaki today is tiny, home to only a few dozen residents, sitting at a modest altitude just above the beach it shares its name with.

A Huge Beach Inside a Sheltered Cove

What distinguishes Pyrgaki most clearly from its neighbours further up the coast is the combination of genuine scale and genuine shelter. The beach sits within a small cove that is well protected from the northern winds, meaning the meltemi that regularly disturbs open, west-facing beaches elsewhere on this coast reaches Pyrgaki only in a much reduced form, if at all. Within that sheltered setting, a continuous strip of fine-grained golden sand extends for a little over a kilometre, with azure, clear water and a seabed that shelves gently rather than dropping away, making it a genuinely comfortable beach for children and confident swimmers alike. The overall impression, repeated across most descriptions of the beach, is of a huge, open expanse of sand that somehow still manages to feel calm and enclosed at the same time, an unusual combination on an island where large beaches tend to also be exposed ones.

Small, Old Cedar Trees and an Unusual Atmosphere

A scattering of small, old cedar trees, in reality a species of juniper related to the larger Kedrodasos forest further up the coast at Alyko, grows along parts of the beach, adding a distinctive, slightly wild character to the scenery. Several descriptions of Pyrgaki specifically note that the combination of dunes, sparse cedar cover and open sand gives the beach an atmosphere more reminiscent of the North African Mediterranean coast than a typical Aegean beach, an impression shared with several of Naxos's other southwestern beaches, Kastraki and Sahara among them, all of which have attracted similar comparisons at different points along the same stretch of coast.

A Cove Shaped for Cliff Diving

Beyond ordinary swimming, Pyrgaki's cove has also developed something of a local reputation among visitors specifically interested in cliff jumping and diving, thanks to the particular morphology of the rock formations around the bay, which offer a range of jumping heights into clear, deep water. This kind of informal diving is generally considered suitable only for older children and confident swimmers, and, as with any similar activity, is best avoided when a southern wind is blowing into the cove, since conditions can change the character of the water quickly under those circumstances.

Minimal by Design

Pyrgaki remains largely unorganised: visitors should expect, at most, a small number of umbrellas and sunbeds available locally, rather than the fully equipped rental setup found at Naxos's busier central beaches. A small number of tavernas operate in the immediate area, generally offering simple food, coffee and drinks rather than an extensive menu, alongside a modest and growing number of newly built hotels, rooms and studios in the surrounding area, several of which take advantage of genuinely fine views over the beach and cove below. Visitors intending to spend a full day here are generally better served bringing their own shade and provisions as a backup rather than relying entirely on what's available locally.

Getting There

Pyrgaki is reached from Naxos Town via the village of Vivlos, following an asphalt road for almost the entire distance before a final short stretch, roughly a kilometre, continues on a well-maintained but unpaved road to the beach itself; the route is easily manageable by ordinary car or motorbike. Public bus service to Pyrgaki is limited, in some accounts running only once a day, making a rental car considerably more practical for most visitors than relying on the timetable. The final approach to Pyrgaki lies about a kilometre beyond Alyko along the same general coastal route already used to reach that beach and the rest of Naxos's southwestern shore.

Nearby: Agiassos and the True End of the Road

Beyond Pyrgaki lies Agiassos, generally considered the final beach reachable by car on this stretch of Naxos's coast, and somewhat more developed than Pyrgaki itself: a long stretch of sand with shallow water, a single hotel, a scattering of rooms, studios and apartments, and a small mini-market, though, like Pyrgaki, without loungers or umbrellas available for rent. Agiassos can be reached either by following a signposted dirt road onward from Pyrgaki or via a separate route through the inland village of Sagri. Working back up the coast from Pyrgaki, Alyko, Glyfada, Kastraki and Mikri Vigla, all covered elsewhere on this blog, complete the long, increasingly developed run of beaches leading back toward Naxos Town.

 

Most visitors to Naxos's southwestern coast turn back somewhere around Alyko, whether from the walk to reach Kedros or simply because the road starts to feel like it's leading somewhere less certain. Pyrgaki rewards anyone who keeps going a little further: a genuinely enormous beach, sheltered enough to feel private despite its size, with cedar shade, a good cove for jumping into deep water, and, more often than not, hardly another visitor in sight.
