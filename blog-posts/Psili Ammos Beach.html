Psili Ammos: Naxos's Fine Sand, Far From Everything

An hour's drive from Chora, over the island's mountainous spine and down a coast road shared more often with goats than cars, a small cove of cedar trees and pale sand looks out toward Koufonisia. Psili Ammos means exactly what it looks like — fine sand — and it sits about as far from the tourist circuit as Naxos gets.

PSILI AMMOS: NAXOS'S FINE SAND, FAR FROM EVERYTHING

 

Psili Ammos lies on Naxos's eastern coast, in the small hamlet of Kanaki, roughly six kilometres south of the fishing village of Moutsouna and around forty-four to forty-six kilometres from Naxos Town, a drive that typically takes well over an hour along the winding mountain road through Chalki, Filoti and Apeiranthos. It is, by a considerable margin, the most remote beach covered anywhere in this series, and it looks and feels the part: a modest, sheltered cove of pale sand backed by a cedar forest and rolling dunes, facing east across open water toward the islands of Koufonisia and Donousa rather than back toward the crowded western coast most visitors never leave.

A Name That Simply Describes Itself

The name translates directly from Greek as "fine sand," and it is exactly, unpretentiously accurate: the beach is composed of soft, fine-grained golden sand, without the coarser texture found at several of Naxos's western beaches. Locally, the beach is also sometimes referred to by the name of the hamlet it belongs to, Kanaki, one of a handful of small settlements strung along this stretch of the eastern coast that exist primarily to serve the farming and fishing communities of the interior rather than tourism.

A Different Naxos Entirely

Reaching Psili Ammos means crossing the island's mountainous spine, and the landscape on both sides of that crossing is markedly different from the resort coastline further west. Visitor accounts of the drive and the area consistently describe a genuinely rural, working landscape: small farmhouses, olive groves, and free-roaming goats that outnumber tourists for long stretches of the route. This is not incidental scenery but a fair reflection of what the eastern coast actually is, an area historically tied to the mountain village of Apeiranthos and its farming and, formerly, mining economy, rather than to the beach-resort development that has shaped so much of the west.

Cedar, Dunes and Shallow, Clear Water

The beach itself sits where a stand of cedar trees, in reality a species of juniper found at several points along Naxos's coastline, gives way to sand dunes and then to the shore. Those cedars offer welcome shade, though visitor accounts note that shaded spots can run out during the busiest hours of the day, and packing a beach umbrella as a backup is generally recommended. The water is shallow, warm and consistently described as crystal clear, making Psili Ammos a genuinely good option for families with small children despite its remoteness, and the beach is wide enough, by most accounts, that finding space is rarely difficult even when others are present. Multiple independent visitor reviews rank Psili Ammos among the very best beaches on Naxos, a notable claim on an island with no shortage of competition for that title.

Finding It: A Signpost and a Short Walk

Psili Ammos is not organised in any sense: there are no sunbeds, umbrellas or beach bars, and visitors should arrive prepared with their own food, water and shade. Access has improved in recent years with the construction of a paved road for most of the route, though the final approach still requires some care. The most reliable way to find the beach is to navigate toward a signpost for Theonis Villas along the coastal road; from there, a short footpath, generally described as no more than a few hundred metres, leads directly down to the shore. Signage beyond this point is limited, and the beach itself is not easily visible from the road, so driving slowly and watching for the sign is worthwhile. Visitors based in Moutsouna itself, a short distance to the north, can also reach Psili Ammos by bicycle along the coastal road, or by hiring a small local boat, arranged much like a taxi, for those who would rather approach by sea.

Moutsouna: The Gateway, and a Monument to Emery

Any visit to Psili Ammos effectively begins in Moutsouna, the small fishing village that serves as the eastern coast's only real settlement of any size and the last reliable place for food, fuel or assistance before the more isolated hamlets further south. Moutsouna's own history gives the wider area much of its distinct character: until the early 1980s, it functioned as the export port for emery, an abrasive mineral mined in the mountains above and used worldwide for polishing metal, glass and stone. The emery was carried down from the mines by an aerial cableway, a genuinely ambitious piece of engineering completed in the late 1920s, and the rusted remains of that cableway, its loading cranes, and the old stone quay are still visible in the village today, now preserved as a monument to the island's modern industrial history. The contrast between this weathered industrial past and the quiet, clear-watered coves that follow it southward gives the whole area a character quite unlike anywhere on Naxos's western coast.

Getting There

From Naxos Town, the most direct route to Psili Ammos runs via Chalki, Filoti and Apeiranthos before descending a narrow, winding mountain road to Moutsouna, and then continuing south along a coastal road, now paved for most of its length, through the hamlets of Ligaridia and Kanaki. The full journey covers upward of forty-four kilometres and is best undertaken by rental car, since public bus service to this side of the island is limited and, in many years, does not operate outside the height of summer. In the event of a genuine emergency, Apeiranthos, roughly nineteen kilometres away, offers the nearest meaningful services; anything more serious requires the drive back to Naxos Town.

Nearby: The Rest of the Eastern Coast

South of Psili Ammos, the same coastal road continues through the hamlet of Klidos toward Panermos Bay, where the road finally ends at a sheltered harbour and a further attractive beach; the low hill above that bay holds the remains of Korfari ton Amygdalon, the fortified Early Cycladic settlement covered elsewhere in this series, adding a genuine archaeological layer to a coastline more often visited for its scenery alone. North of Psili Ammos, Moutsouna itself offers its own two small beaches, Agios Ioannis and Tigani, alongside fish tavernas serving the day's catch, making it an easy, worthwhile stop on the way to or from Psili Ammos rather than merely a waypoint to pass through.

 

Most of the beaches covered in this series reward a short walk past the crowd. Psili Ammos rewards something bigger: a genuine crossing of the island, over its mountains and away from the coastline most visitors never think to leave. What waits on the other side is fine sand, cedar shade, a view toward islands few package holidays ever mention, and a working history of emery and goats that has nothing to do with tourism at all. For a sense of the Naxos that exists independently of its beaches, this is very often where that sense actually begins.
