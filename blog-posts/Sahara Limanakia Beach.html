SAHARA (LIMANAKIA): THE SHELTERED SIDE OF MIKRI VIGLA

 

Sahara sits on Naxos's western coast, roughly twelve kilometres south of Naxos Town, forming the southern half of the beach complex better known collectively as Mikri Vigla. Where the northern beach, Parthena, has built an international reputation on wind, Sahara exists specifically because the same headland that generates Parthena's famous gusts blocks them almost entirely from reaching the other side. The result is one of the calmest, most consistently swimmable stretches of coast on the island, popular enough with visitors seeking exactly that calm to have earned two separate names in everyday use.

Two Names, Two Different Stories

The beach is known locally by two distinct nicknames, each pointing to a different feature of the place. "Sahara" refers simply to the sand itself: a long, largely uninterrupted expanse of fine, pale grains that, in scale and colour, reminded enough visitors of a desert landscape for the name to stick. "Limanakia," or in its singular form "Limanaki," means "little harbour," and refers to something more specific: the irregular, wind-carved rocks of the Mikri Vigla cape curve around part of this southern shore in a way that forms a natural, sheltered harbour, historically used by small local fishing boats. Both names remain in common use today, sometimes applied to the beach as a whole and sometimes, in the plural "Limanakia," to the smaller individual coves and inlets along its rockier northern edge.

A Kilometre of Gently Sloping Sand

The beach runs for approximately one kilometre of soft, pale sand, fronting water that begins shallow near the shoreline and deepens gradually enough to suit confident swimmers and cautious waders alike. The water itself is consistently described as crystal clear, running through pale aquamarine close to the beach into deeper turquoise further out, with a sandy seabed that shelves evenly rather than dropping away sharply. That combination, gentle depth and genuine shelter from the region's prevailing wind, makes Sahara one of the more consistently recommended beaches on Naxos for families with young children, and for anyone simply looking for a long, uninterrupted swim rather than a battle with chop and current.

Kavos: A Smaller Cove in Between

Between Sahara and Parthena, tucked into the fold of the headland itself, lies Kavos, a small, quiet cove that functions as a third, even more sheltered option within the same complex. Kavos is not organised in any formal sense, with no sunbeds or facilities of its own, and it is particularly favoured on days when Parthena's wind is blowing hard enough to make even Sahara feel slightly disturbed at its edges, since the cove's tighter enclosure offers protection more complete still. Visitors exploring Mikri Vigla on foot can move between all three beaches, Parthena, Kavos and Sahara, in a single unhurried walk around the headland.

Rocky Corners, Snorkelling and Ancient Engravings

While the main body of Sahara is sandy, its northern edge, closer to the headland itself, turns rockier, with the same wind-sculpted stone that forms the sheltered harbour also creating genuinely rewarding conditions for snorkelling among the crevices and shallows near the cape. The hillsides around the headland carry a further, less commonly noted detail: ancient inscriptions and engraved markings, weathered by centuries of exposure to the wind, visible to those who take the time to look closely at the rock faces rather than simply passing along the sand. Naxos holds a genuine, if scattered, tradition of this kind of open-air rock art, most famously at Korfi Aroniou on the island's southeastern coast, and the engravings around Mikri Vigla's cape add a small, little-publicised extension of that same broader story to this stretch of coastline.

A Salt Lake Turned Wetland

A short distance inland from the shore, hidden behind the dunes and low ground of the headland, lies a small seasonal lake with its own working history: for generations, it was used to harvest salt, one of Naxos's principal export goods in earlier centuries, in the same tradition that once shaped the salt pans still visible behind Agios Prokopios and Glyfada further up this same coastline. That industrial use has long since ended, and the lake today functions instead as a natural wetland, attracting a range of migratory birds that pass through or pause here during their seasonal journeys — a quiet ecological postscript to what was once a genuinely practical local resource.

Amenities: Present, But Not Abundant

Sahara is at least partially organised, with sunbeds and umbrellas available for rent along parts of the beach and a handful of tavernas and restaurants operating nearby, several with genuine sea views and a solid, unpretentious local menu. That said, visitors should not expect the same density of facilities found at Naxos's busier central beaches: given the beach's popularity relative to its size, sunbed availability can run short during the peak weeks of July and August, and travellers who value certainty over spontaneity are generally advised to arrive early in the day or bring their own shade as a backup. The broader Mikri Vigla area, on the Parthena side of the headland, offers a wider range of accommodation, sports centres and dining for those staying in the area longer term.

Getting There

Sahara is reached from Naxos Town via the coastal road running south through Agios Prokopios, Agia Anna and Plaka, covering roughly twelve kilometres and taking around twenty minutes by car. It shares its access points with the rest of the Mikri Vigla complex, and taxis, rental cars and, less frequently, public buses all serve the wider area. Once parked or dropped near Mikri Vigla, reaching Sahara specifically simply means walking around, rather than over, the headland to its southern side.

Nearby: The Rest of Mikri Vigla and Beyond

Sahara forms one half of the wider Mikri Vigla beach complex covered in more detail elsewhere on this blog, with Parthena and its internationally known windsurfing and kitesurfing conditions lying just across the headland to the north, and Kavos tucked in between the two. Further south along the same coastline, Kastraki, Glyfada and the protected cedar forest of Alyko continue Naxos's long southwestern shore, making Sahara a natural, easily combined stop for visitors working their way along this entire stretch of coast.

 

Most descriptions of Mikri Vigla lead with the wind, since that is what put the headland on the international map. Sahara is the quieter half of the same story: the side of the cape where the wind simply doesn't arrive, where a working salt industry has become a bird sanctuary, and where a short walk over rock brings both good snorkelling and a scatter of engravings that few visitors ever think to look for. For anyone drawn to Mikri Vigla for reasons other than a kite line, this is the beach that actually answers the call.
