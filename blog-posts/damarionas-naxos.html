Damarionas, Naxos: A Traditional Village of the Tragea Valley

Damarionas lies on the southern side of the Tragea valley, roughly 16 kilometres from Naxos Town and little more than a kilometre from Halki. Officially designated a traditional settlement, it is known for whitewashed houses, paved lanes, a series of covered passages called stegasta, old family mansions, and the nearby cave church of Panagia tis Spilias. Its permanent population is estimated at around 350.

DAMARIONAS, NAXOS: A TRADITIONAL VILLAGE OF THE TRAGEA VALLEY

 

Damarionas is a small farming village in central Naxos, set on the southern edge of the Tragea (Drymalia) valley. It is one of several settlements around the valley’s olive groves, alongside Halki, Filoti, Kaloxylos and Damalas, and it is distinguished from them by an unusually intact village core: stone-paved lanes, arched passages, mansions of local families and squares shaded by old plane trees. The Greek state has recognised this character by listing Damarionas as a traditional settlement.

Location and Setting

Damarionas stands at an altitude of about 230 metres and is roughly 15 to 17 kilometres from Naxos Town, depending on the route and the source. Neighbouring settlements are close: Vourvouria is about 700 metres away, Damalas about 1.2 kilometres and Halki about 1.4 kilometres. The area is sometimes described as mountainous in character despite its modest elevation, and the surrounding landscape is one of terraced fields, olive groves, vineyards, citrus trees and vegetable plots. Administratively, the village belongs to the Drymalia municipal unit within the municipality of Naxos and Lesser Cyclades.

The village’s name has no documented origin, but local tradition links it to Damaris, the Athenian woman recorded in the Acts of the Apostles as one of the early converts to Christianity made by St. Paul in Athens. The tradition holds that she is assumed to have spread the new faith among the inhabitants. This is a tradition, not a historical record, and it is presented as such by the main local sources.

A Village Moved Inland

Local accounts agree on a broad outline of the settlement’s history. The first inhabitants are said to have lived near the south-western coast, in the Agiassos area, and to have moved inland because of pirate raids, which affected the Cyclades for centuries. They first settled at a location called Roalidia, on the slopes beneath the Apalirou castle, and later, from around 1400, at the present site. Dates in these sources are approximate and rest largely on local tradition rather than excavated evidence, so they are best read as estimates.

That pattern, a move from the coast to a sheltered inland site, is common across the Cyclades, and it explains the compact, inward-facing layout of the village. Demographically, Damarionas has followed the island’s wider rural trend: one local source gives a population of 522 in 1981 and 313 in 1991, with the present figure estimated at about 350. Including the surrounding hamlets of Vourvouria, Damalas, Agiassos and Pirgaki, the wider community is estimated at around 550 permanent residents.

Stegasta, Mansions and Squares

The architectural interest of Damarionas lies in its village fabric. The settlement is composed of whitewashed, cubic houses and older mansions along narrow lanes paved with stone slabs, with balconies and doorways decorated with plants. Its best-known feature is a set of covered passages known as stegasta, with local sources referring to “the seven stegasta” of the village. These are vaulted or arched corridors built over the lane, joining buildings on either side; they provide shade and shelter and are a characteristic feature of older Naxian settlements, including nearby Filoti.

Local heritage listings single out the mansions of the Promponas, Vassilikoulas and Bakalenas families, and two village squares: Potirou square, centred on a very large old plane tree, and Patelos square, beside the old church of Christ. The church of Agios Georgios is also noted among the older churches in the village centre. Many of the older houses are in the process of restoration, which is a common pattern in villages of this kind.

Churches and Chapels

The parish church is dedicated to the Metamorphosis of the Saviour (the Transfiguration), and the village festival is held on 6 August, with the celebration beginning on the evening before. A second church, dedicated to the Panagia, stands at the edge of the village and celebrates on 15 August; local tradition says that its construction began during the Ottoman period and was completed only slowly, because villagers were occupied with agricultural work.

The wider area has many older chapels. The best known is Panagia tis Spilias (“Our Lady of the Cave”), a small church built into a cave in the Xerokampos area near the village. Visitors find icons and votive offerings placed beneath a large natural rock formation. A local legend, retold in several guides, says that a girl from a local noble family, fleeing armed pirates, found the cave, which contained two icons including one of the Virgin, and later returned to build a church there. As with the Damaris tradition, this is folklore and should be read as such. Further chapels are found in the Baouzi area, and the early Christian church of Kaloritsa is described as built in a cave on the western side of Mount Ai Lias.

Monuments and Local Memory

The village keeps several small monuments. These include a bust of Ioannis Paparrigopoulos, a native of Damarionas and a member of the Filiki Eteria, the secret society that prepared the Greek Revolution of 1821; a monument to villagers who died in Greece’s national wars; and a bust of the local doctor Georgios Veronis near the village entrance. Together with the preserved architecture, they record the village’s place in the wider national story rather than only its agricultural one.

Local Life, Food and Agiassos

The local economy is based on farming and animal husbandry, and a modern olive mill operates in the village. Guides mention two local specialities: handmade pies filled with wild greens in thin pastry, and citron preserve, a jam-like sweet made from citron, the same fruit used for the island’s kitron liqueur. The area between the village and the south-western coast is dry, open country used for grazing, and local shepherds are known for cheese-making.

Agiassos, a sandy beach on the south-west coast, is administratively and historically associated with Damarionas, since the village’s first inhabitants are said to have lived there. It lies much farther away than the other places mentioned here, around 23 kilometres from Naxos Town by one account, and is generally described as a quiet beach with calm, clear water.

Visiting Damarionas

Damarionas is reached by car or taxi from Naxos Town along the main road into the Tragea valley, in about 25 to 30 minutes in normal traffic; public bus services to the Tragea villages exist but timetables change seasonally and should be checked locally. The village is best explored on foot, and the paved lanes are uneven, so flat, sturdy footwear is advisable. Because Damarionas is a living village, the houses and mansions are private homes, and visits are limited to the lanes, squares and churches. Churches may be locked outside service times.

The village combines naturally with Halki (about 1.4 kilometres away, see the separate Halki guide), Damalas, Vourvouria and the walking paths between them, which cross olive groves and pass several Byzantine and post-Byzantine churches. For the wider Venetian background to the region, see the guide to the Kastro of Naxos. Summer afternoons are hot and unshaded outside the village, so late spring and early autumn are generally the more comfortable seasons for walking.

Quick Answers

Where is Damarionas? It is in the southern part of the Tragea valley in central Naxos, about 16 kilometres from Naxos Town and 1.4 kilometres from Halki.

What is Damarionas known for? It is known for its status as a traditional settlement, its covered stegasta passages, its old mansions and squares, and the cave church of Panagia tis Spilias.

How long is a visit? A walk through the village takes about one to two hours, and longer if combined with Panagia tis Spilias or a walk to Halki.

How is the village name explained? Local tradition derives it from Damaris the Athenian, an early Christian convert. This is a tradition and is not historically documented.

 

Damarionas is not a monumental site. Its value lies in the continuity of a working village: a layout shaped by the move inland from pirate-exposed coasts, a street fabric preserved well enough to earn official protection, and a farming economy still tied to the surrounding valley.
