Iraklia: The Small Cyclades' Quietest Island

Roughly 150 people live year-round on the largest island of the Small Cyclades, in two villages connected by a stone footpath, above a cave so vast that local legend once placed a Cyclops inside it. Iraklia has spent most of its history being genuinely difficult to reach. It has spent the years since simply declining to change because of it.

IRAKLIA: THE SMALL CYCLADES' QUIETEST ISLAND

 

Iraklia sits at the western edge of the Small Cyclades, the loose chain of minor islands strung between Naxos and Amorgos, and holds a slightly contradictory distinction within that group: the largest of the Small Cyclades by land area, at just under eighteen square kilometres, and yet the smallest in year-round population, home to roughly 150 permanent residents according to the most recent census. That combination, plenty of space and remarkably few people using it, defines almost everything about a visit here.

Named for Hercules

The island's name derives, according to Greek tradition, from Hercules, and it has been known under some form of that name since antiquity. A French archaeologist working in the area in the mid-1890s identified a fragmentary inscription confirming the island's ancient status as its own polis, a formally recognised city-state, small evidence of a settled and organised community here well before the term "Small Cyclades" existed as a way of grouping it with its neighbours.

Getting There From Naxos

Iraklia is reached from Naxos Town by ferry, either the well-loved local Skopelitis, connecting the Small Cyclades on a route that takes roughly ninety minutes and runs daily except Sunday, or a faster conventional ferry line covering the crossing in around an hour, three times a week. Both options are straightforward and inexpensive, though visitors should check current schedules carefully given the more limited frequency of service to this particular island compared with its better-known neighbour Koufonisia, covered elsewhere on this blog.

Two Villages, and a Village Built to Hide

Iraklia has effectively two working settlements. Agios Georgios, the port, is a small cluster of houses around a genuinely sheltered harbour, its own tamarisk-shaded beach a short walk from the boats. Panagia, the island's chora, sits inland on high ground, considerably smaller and quieter than the port village, its position deliberately chosen: like Halki and several other historic Naxian villages covered elsewhere on this blog, Panagia was built specifically where it could not be seen from the sea, protecting its residents from the pirate raids that repeatedly threatened this entire stretch of the Aegean for centuries. A stone footpath through terraced countryside still connects the two villages today, a pleasant walk in its own right rather than merely a practical route. A third settlement, Agios Athanasios, survives only as a scatter of abandoned stone houses on the island's western side, its traditional Cycladic architecture intact but its population long since gone.

Five Thousand Years of Quiet Habitation

Iraklia has been inhabited since early antiquity, with archaeological traces reaching back to the Early Cycladic period, the same broad cultural horizon responsible for the marble figurines and fortified settlements covered extensively elsewhere on this blog in relation to Naxos itself. The island holds one particularly enigmatic piece of prehistoric evidence: a set of rock paintings known as the Bousoules, estimated at roughly five thousand years old and possibly used as orientation markers, whose precise meaning and purpose remain genuinely unresolved. In more recent centuries, Iraklia belonged to the Hozoviotissa Monastery on neighbouring Amorgos, a reminder of how thoroughly monastic institutions once shaped land ownership across this part of the Aegean, and, like most of the Small Cyclades, it endured repeated pirate raids that at times forced its population to abandon the island altogether. For much of the twentieth century, Iraklia remained reachable only by irregular wooden boats; the launch of the Skopelitis ferry in the 1980s marked the real beginning of its gradual, still fairly limited opening to outside visitors.

A Hellenistic Fort Above Livadi

Above Livadi Bay, the island's best-preserved archaeological site occupies a hilltop known simply as Kastro: the remains of a fortified acropolis dating to the fourth through second centuries BC, its walls, house foundations, courtyards and threshing floors still traceable across the ridge, alongside the ruins of at least one temple dedicated to Zeus. Excavation has identified several distinct layers of occupation here, from prehistoric fortification through to medieval reuse, when islanders returned to the same defensible high ground as a refuge during the pirate raids of later centuries. The view from the site, out across Livadi Bay to the small islet of Venetiko beyond, remains one of the more rewarding vantage points on the island.

The Cave of Agios Ioannis

Iraklia's single most celebrated attraction lies inland, roughly a ninety-minute walk from Panagia through herb-scented scrubland: the Cave of Agios Ioannis, one of the largest caves anywhere in Greece, its chambers covering an area of some two thousand square metres and filled with an elaborate array of stalactites, stalagmites and columns, along with a rare mineral deposit known locally as "cave-milk." Discovered by a shepherd and still without any artificial lighting, a full exploration takes more than two hours and requires visitors to bring their own torches; a local guide is strongly advisable both for safety and for the fuller context of the cave's long association with local legend, since a smaller, open cave directly opposite is traditionally linked to Polyphemus, the Cyclops of Homer's Odyssey, said to have grazed his flocks in the area. Every year on August 28th, the Feast of Agios Ioannis draws pilgrims and visitors alike for one of the more extraordinary religious festivals anywhere in Greece: a full liturgy conducted inside the cave's dark chambers, followed by music and dancing back in the village once the ceremony concludes.

Beaches for Every Kind of Effort

Livadi, roughly twenty minutes on foot from the port, is Iraklia's largest and most popular beach: golden sand, shallow, tamarisk-shaded water well suited to families, and open views across to Venetiko and Schinousa, with a long-standing tolerance for free camping and informal nudism at its quieter edges. The small beach at Agios Georgios itself, right by the port, offers the easiest option for visitors without transport, with a scatter of tavernas immediately behind it. Tourkopigado, a sheltered, fjord-like cove of white pebbles reached by a 2.3-kilometre trail from Panagia, rewards the walk with some of the calmest water on the island, while Vorini Spilia, a quieter sandy beach north of the port, suits visitors looking for genuine solitude without needing a boat.

Two further beaches reward the extra effort of a boat trip. Alimia, a mixed sand-and-pebble cove, has become something of a favourite among snorkellers thanks to an unusual attraction resting just beneath the surface: the wreck of a German seaplane shot down during the Second World War, still visible from a passing boat on a calm day. Karvounolakos, further south, is smaller and more secluded still, framed by dramatic, cave-pocked rock formations in shades of brown and green, its coarse pebbles and clear water reachable only by private or local boat.

Eight Trails and a View From Papas Hill

Iraklia is threaded by eight signposted hiking trails, the most rewarding of which links Panagia directly to the Cave of Agios Ioannis. From Papas Hill, the island's highest point, a genuinely expansive panorama takes in a remarkable stretch of the surrounding Aegean, from Naxos, Paros and Antiparos to Ios, Amorgos, Santorini and the scattered smaller islands of the Small Cyclades group in between, a rare single vantage point over so much of the same central Cycladic geography covered elsewhere on this blog.

A Place Defined by What It Lacks

Iraklia's character is shaped less by any single attraction than by the absence of the things that define busier islands nearby: no real nightlife, no significant crowds, and, for most of the year, no particular urgency about anything at all. Around fifteen tavernas, concentrated mainly in Agios Georgios and the main square of Panagia, serve simple, fresh local food, grilled fish, slow-cooked goat, homemade cheese and fava, the kind of meal that tends to stretch into a long, unhurried evening rather than a quick stop between activities.

Planning the Visit

A single day from Naxos allows time to see Agios Georgios, walk to Livadi for a swim, and, with an early start, make the return hike to the Cave of Agios Ioannis, though fitting the cave in comfortably alongside everything else in one day is ambitious given the walking time involved. Visitors with more flexibility are better served by at least one overnight stay, allowing the cave hike, a boat trip to Alimia or Karvounolakos, and a proper look at both villages without rushing. Accommodation on Iraklia remains genuinely limited by comparison with its neighbours, and booking ahead for July and August is essential rather than optional.

Nearby: The Rest of the Small Cyclades

Iraklia sits within the same short ferry network as Koufonisia and Schinousa, both part of the Small Cyclades group and reachable on the same local routes, making it entirely possible to combine two or more of these islands into a single longer excursion from a Naxos base. For visitors already drawn to this blog's coverage of Naxos's own Early Cycladic archaeology, Iraklia's Bousoules rock paintings and its Hellenistic fort at Livadi add a further, quieter chapter to that same long, still only partly understood story of early Aegean settlement.

 

Most islands this size have, by now, found some way to be discovered. Iraklia has mostly avoided it, not through any deliberate resistance but simply because the ferry takes longer, the cave requires a torch and a real walk, and the beaches that reward the most effort are also the ones no road will ever reach. For visitors who have already spent a few days on Naxos's wider stretch of coastline and are looking for exactly the opposite of a crowd, this is very often where that search quietly ends.
