Apano Kastro: The Fortress Even Barbarossa Couldn't Take

Between the green valley of Potamia and the olive-covered plateau of Tragaea, a rocky hilltop rises 420 metres above Naxos's fertile interior. For the Venetian dukes who ruled the island for over three centuries, this was the fortress of last resort — strong enough to turn back the most feared pirate admiral in the Mediterranean, and never actually taken by force in three and a half centuries of continuous use.

APANO KASTRO: THE FORTRESS EVEN BARBAROSSA COULDN'T TAKE

 

Naxos has two Venetian-era castles bearing the name Kastro, and it's worth being clear from the outset which is which. The Kastro of Naxos Town, covered elsewhere in this series, was the Duchy's seat of government, a genuinely lived-in citadel that grew into the historic core of Chora itself. Apano Kastro — literally the "Upper Castle" — is something different: a remote, purely defensive hilltop stronghold deep in the island's mountainous interior, between the fertile valley of Potamia and the olive-terraced plateau of Tragaea, reachable today only on foot. The Venetians who built it called it Castello d'Alto, to distinguish it from Chora's Kato Kastro, the "Lower Castle." For three and a half centuries, it was, by most accounts, the strongest inland fortress on Naxos.

Fortified Long Before the Venetians Arrived

The strategic value of this particular hilltop was recognised long before Marco Sanudo ever set foot on Naxos. Substantial remains of a much older wall, built from large, precisely cut rectangular stone blocks in a style unmistakably belonging to the Classical era, are still visible at the site today, layered beneath and around the later medieval construction — clear evidence that this same hill served a defensive purpose well over a thousand years before the Venetians took any interest in it. What visitors see standing today, however, is overwhelmingly the product of a very different period: the 13th and 14th centuries, when the site was reconstructed and fortified by Naxos's new Venetian rulers into the imposing stronghold whose ruins survive now.

A Disputed Origin Story

Exactly when, and by whom, the Venetian-era Apano Kastro was built remains a genuinely open question, with several competing traditions recorded by different sources. One account holds that Marco Sanudo himself used the hilltop as his very first base immediately after conquering Naxos in 1207, implying an existing, habitable fortification already stood there at the time — before he relocated his capital down to the coast at what is now Chora. A second, more widely repeated tradition credits his grandson, Marco II Sanudo, with substantially rebuilding and fortifying the site toward the end of the 13th century, for two connected reasons: defence against the frequent pirate raids that plagued the Aegean throughout this period, and, just as importantly, intimidation of a local island population not always pleased with their Venetian feudal overlords. A third theory places the fortress's major construction, or a significant later renovation, closer to the end of the 14th century, driven by the rising threat of Ottoman naval power in the Aegean. The truth may well combine elements of all three — an older core adapted and strengthened across successive Venetian generations as the nature of the threat facing Naxos itself continued to change.

Two Fortified Rings, Two Very Different Purposes

Whatever its exact building history, the fortress that emerged followed a deliberate, socially telling design: two concentric fortification lines, rather than one. The outer, lower circuit, tracing the base of the hill, was intended for the general local population, offering a place of collective refuge during a raid; the inner, upper enclosure, set higher and more strongly defended, was reserved specifically for the Venetian lords themselves — a physical, architectural expression of exactly the same feudal hierarchy that governed daily life on Naxos throughout the Duchy's existence. A horseshoe-shaped bastion, evidently added at a later date to accommodate firearms, shows the fortress continuing to be actively adapted and maintained well into the age of gunpowder warfare, long after its earliest medieval walls were first raised. Remains of walls, battlements, elite residential structures traditionally described as "palaces," and several cisterns for storing water during a siege are all still visible across the site today, along with two Byzantine-era chapels, dedicated to the Virgin Mary Kastriani and to Saint George, built directly against the fortress walls.

Tellingly, despite its scale and evident sophistication, Apano Kastro never developed into a genuine permanent settlement in its own right. It functioned throughout its working life purely as a refuge and stronghold, occupied in force only when danger actually threatened, rather than as somewhere people made their ordinary homes — a role quite different from the Kastro of Naxos Town, which grew, over the same centuries, into a genuinely lived-in neighbourhood that remains inhabited today.

A Dark Local Name

The wider area around the fortress carries a name that hints at a harsher local history than the castle's successful sieges alone might suggest. Locals have long referred to the district as "Koutsocherado," a name some accounts connect to a massacre of the area's residents by pirate raiders, and the surrounding locality is also sometimes simply called "the Slaves" — a grim likely reference to the human cost of the raiding and slave-taking that made a genuine fortress necessary on this hilltop in the first place. Two ancient cemeteries in the vicinity, including a documented Geometric-period necropolis near the village of Tsikalario, add a further, much older layer of burial history to a landscape that has clearly mattered, for one grave reason or another, across an exceptionally long span of Naxian history.

A Fortress That Held, Almost Every Time

Apano Kastro's real historical importance lies in how consistently it did its job. In 1537, the Ottoman admiral and pirate Hayreddin Barbarossa, fresh from plundering his way across much of the Aegean that same year, laid siege to Apano Kastro and failed to take it — a genuinely significant achievement, given Barbarossa's fearsome reputation and the wider devastation he inflicted elsewhere in the region that year. Naxos itself did eventually fall under Ottoman influence, but not through Apano Kastro's capture: the Venetian duke Ioannis IV Crispo instead surrendered the keys to the coastal Kastro of Chora, leaving the mountain fortress technically unconquered even as the wider Duchy's independence effectively ended.

The castle continued to serve the Venetian administration for generations afterward. In 1620, it was here that Angelos Sanudo organised his defence against a rebellion by the inhabitants of the Tragaea region — a reminder of Apano Kastro's dual role, throughout its working life, as both a shield against external raiders and a tool for the Venetian nobility's continued control over an often resentful local population. The fortress finally fell, remarkably for the only time in its recorded history, in 1678, when it was captured by the notorious French corsair Hugues de Crevelliers — not to an imperial navy or a rival Great Power, but to an individual pirate captain, a fittingly ironic end for a stronghold originally built, at least in part, to keep pirates out.

A Note on Its Predecessor: Apaliros Castle

Apano Kastro's story only makes full sense alongside that of an earlier Byzantine fortress a short distance away: Apaliros Castle, between the villages of Sagri and Agiassos, which served as the administrative capital of Byzantine Naxos until the very arrival of Marco Sanudo in 1207. According to the traditional account, Sanudo besieged Apaliros for forty days, at one point burning his own ships to force his wavering mercenaries to keep fighting rather than retreat, before finally taking it from the Genoese pirates then occupying it. Rather than reoccupy this older inland capital, Sanudo chose to build his new seat of power at Chora instead, close to the sea in the Venetian tradition, and Apaliros was subsequently neglected, and by some accounts deliberately destroyed, to deny it to any future rival power. Apano Kastro's later construction, whichever Sanudo actually built it, effectively picked up Apaliros's older strategic role — an inland stronghold for genuine emergencies — while Chora took on the everyday business of governing the island.

Visiting Apano Kastro Today

Apano Kastro sits at 420 metres above sea level, with commanding views over both the Potamia valley to the west and the Tragaea plateau to the east — a genuinely dramatic panorama that makes clear, at a glance, exactly why this hilltop mattered to every power that ever controlled it. The most popular approach is a well-marked hiking trail beginning at the village of Tsikalario, a walk that takes in the Geometric-era necropolis en route and climbs through typical Naxian upland scenery before reaching the ruins; the path is not especially difficult by Naxos hiking standards, but does involve a genuine, sustained climb rewarded, on arrival, with some of the best inland views on the island. Alternative approaches are also possible from the villages of Potamia and Chalkio for those looking to combine the castle with a longer day exploring Naxos's mountainous centre.

There is no ticket booth, entrance fee or fixed opening hours; the site is an open ruin in open countryside, freely accessible at any time. Sturdy footwear and, in the warmer months, an early start are worth planning around, since the exposed hilltop and the walk itself offer little shade along the way.

Nearby: Naxos's Layered Defensive Landscape

Apano Kastro sits at the centre of Naxos's mountainous interior, within reach of several of the villages that make up the fertile Tragaea region, and pairs naturally with a visit to Bazeos Tower and the Temple of Demeter, both a reasonable drive away in the same general part of the island. Together with the Kastro of Naxos Town and the far older Hellenistic-era Cheimarros Tower, elsewhere in this series, Apano Kastro completes a genuinely striking picture of Naxos as an island that has, across roughly two and a half thousand years, repeatedly needed to defend itself — from Hellenistic-era warfare, through Byzantine and Venetian rivalry, to some of the most feared pirate captains the Mediterranean ever produced.

 

Most visitors to Naxos never make it up to Apano Kastro, and in a sense that's exactly in keeping with the fortress's original purpose: a place built deliberately away from the coast, away from the crowds, meant to be reached only when genuinely necessary. For anyone willing to make the climb today, what remains is less a monument to Venetian grandeur than to sheer defensive stubbornness — a hilltop that held, against pirates, rebels and empires alike, for three and a half centuries, and gave up its keys, when it finally did, to a single corsair rather than a conquering fleet.
